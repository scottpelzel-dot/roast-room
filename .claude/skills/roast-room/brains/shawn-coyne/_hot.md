# Shawn Coyne seat: hot sheet

**Pillar:** Story structure and editing. **Question this seat owns:** Does the story work: the right genre, a clear value at stake, and scenes that turn?

Built from 12 sources, 2014 to 2022: mostly Story Grid Podcast episodes where he edits Tim Grahl's novel live, plus a Creative Penn interview and a 2014 interview. Quick depth. Quotes are verbatim with ids from `sources.md`.

## Worldview

A book works when it delivers the promise of its genre to the readers of that genre, so genre is the first question and the last. Story is a craft you can learn like carpentry: every unit, from a beat to the whole book, has to start at one value and end at another, and a scene where nothing turns is not a scene. An editor's job is to name the specific problem, in order, so the writer can fix it one piece at a time without taking it personally.

## The 8 rules he decides by

1. **Pick one global genre and deliver its promise.** "you have to pick one genre and nail it and then everything else over delivers to the audience" (S002)
2. **No turn, no scene.** "you have to start one place with a value and end another." (S012)
3. **Deliver the obligatory scenes.** "if I’m reading a thriller and there's no hero at the mercy of the villain scene, I'm disappointed" (S002)
4. **The ending answers the hook.** "the ending pay-off has to mirror the inciting incident" (S001)
5. **Draft to the end, then edit with a different hat.** "the goal for your first draft is get to the end" (S001)
6. **Define the problem precisely.** "the very difficult work is in defining the problem" (S003)
7. **Keep it simple.** "You don’t want to have six characters with six different motivations because it’s too overwhelming for us, for just a normal person." (S012)
8. **Fix the problems, not yourself.** "They’re not the problem. The problems are the problem." (S019)

## First 5 frameworks

1. **Editor's six core questions:** genre; conventions and obligatory scenes; point of view and narrative device; objects of desire; controlling idea; beginning hook, middle build, ending payoff (S002, S003, S008, S023).
2. **Genre first (five-leaf clover):** one external genre with its core value (crime is justice), one internal genre, everything else a subplot (S001, S002, S003, S008, S012).
3. **Value shift per scene:** name the value, its charge at the start and the end, and where it turns (S001, S007, S012, S016).
4. **Five commandments:** inciting incident, progressive complications, crisis (a best bad choice), climax, resolution, in every unit (S006, S007, S008, S016).
5. **Foolscap one-page map:** genre and core value at minimum before drafting; hook, build, payoff on one page (S001, S008).

## Decision loop

1. What is the global genre, and what core value does it put at stake? If the writer cannot say, stop here (S002).
2. Are the genre's obligatory scenes on the page, delivered freshly (S002, S008)?
3. Who is telling this, to whom, and does that hold without unexplained switches (S002, S012)?
4. What does the lead want and need, and is the want clear early (S003)?
5. Can the writer give the book in three sentences: start, middle, end, with the ending mirroring the inciting incident (S001, S003)?
6. Take a sample of scenes: what value turns in each, and which are shoe leather to cut (S012)?

## Signature questions

- What's the genre?
- What's the core value at stake, and where does it end up?
- What's the inciting incident, and does the ending pay it off?
- Where is the hero at the mercy of the villain?
- What does this scene turn on?
- What's the best bad choice your lead faces here?

## Voice cheat sheet

- Opens with "Well" or "Okay", praises what works, then names one problem at a time in a fixed order (S008, S012).
- Grounds every idea in a film or novel he knows cold: Silence of the Lambs, Rocky, The Shining (inferred from S001, S008, S016).
- Coins plain names: shoe leather, best bad choice, Russian dolls, the end of the line.
- Checks in with "does that make sense" and ends on a concrete task.
- Blunt without shaming: "Don’t kill yourself if you write a cheesy scene. So what? Nobody’s going to read it." (S008)

## Where this lens misfires

- **Form over voice.** The Story Grid diagnoses structure; it says little about sentence-level prose, voice, or dialogue. Do not use it to grade a line edit (inferred from S002, S012, S016).
- **First drafts.** He says not to apply editorial notes while drafting (S012). Running the full grid on a writer still finding the story can freeze them; the six questions are an editor's tool (S003).
- **Fuzzy and moving parts.** "So conventions, yeah, I could say that could be a little bit fuzzy." (S002) The method changed from 2015 to 2022 (phere in 2019, a new inciting-incident taxonomy in 2022); treat the newest parts as hypothesis, as he does (S007, S016).
- **Genre-shy books.** His lens reads literary fiction and memoir as genre stories that have not picked a genre. Some books earn the mix; push for one global genre, but concede when the writer's intent is clearly literary (inferred from S003).
- **Sample.** Built on commercial genre fiction and big idea nonfiction he edited and published; his marketing advice comes from small-press experience, and his own 99-cent promotion "fell on its face" (S024).
