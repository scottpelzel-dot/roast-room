# Frameworks: Shawn Coyne

Built from the extract notes on 12 sources (S001 to S024): Story Grid Podcast episodes with Tim Grahl (2015 to 2022), a 2015 Creative Penn interview, and a 2014 PDF interview. 18 frameworks, most-used first. "Sources" counts distinct sources where the idea is used (ids listed). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for judging a manuscript, built from his stated tests. They are guidance, not quotes. The Story Grid kept changing from 2015 to 2022; each entry says where.

---

## 1. Genre first (the five-leaf genre clover; external and internal genre)
- **What it is:** Every story belongs to a genre, and the genre is a promise to the reader. Genre is the first question he asks of any manuscript and of himself. He keeps a five-leaf genre clover diagram on his office wall so he does not forget the genres. Genre includes internal plots (maturation, redemption, education, status), not only crime, horror and love.
- **Parts:** Pick one global (external) genre, the movie poster. Name its core value: crime is justice, thriller and action are life and death, horror is life and death pushed to damnation, love story is love and hate, war and performance often shame and honor. Pick an internal genre that carries the change in the lead. Treat any other genre as a subplot that over-delivers. The action genre splits into four types: man against nature, the state, another man, or time (S012).
- **When to use:** First, always. When a draft stalls, when a writer cannot say what the book is, when a writer hides behind a label like literary fiction, when a subplot swamps the main line.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the writer names one global genre in a word or two, its core value, and the internal genre, and the pages signal that genre from scene one (setting and cast set the reader's expectation, S012). 3 if the genre is named but a subplot is what the book is really about. 1 if the writer cannot pick, picks several, or says "literary fiction" to avoid choosing.
- **Quote:** "What's the genre is the best question you as a writer can ever, ever ask yourself, and you need to ask yourself all the time." (S002)
- **Also:** "Because if you know the genre, there’s a lot of different things that you can solve based upon that one simple piece of knowledge." (S020)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S002, S003, S008, S012, S016, S020, S023)

## 2. Story values and the scene turn (value shift, polarity shift)
- **What it is:** A story value is a positive or negative state in life that has gradations (life and death, justice and injustice, love and hate, truth and lies). The scene is the primary unit of story, and a scene must start at one charge of a value and end at another. If nothing shifts, it is not a scene.
- **Parts:** Name the value at stake in the scene (it need not be the global value; a crime novel can have a scene about hope and despair, S001). Mark the charge at the start and at the end. Find where it turns: at the turning point, when the lead's strategies fail (S007). Check the turn is specific: stable to panic, every day to extraordinary. Vague shifts such as known to unknown are weak (S016).
- **When to use:** Every scene, on every pass after the first draft. When a writer says the middle is slow or a chapter feels flat.
- **How to score (guidance):** For a sample of scenes, count how many have a named value with a clear charge change. Most turning: strong. A run of scenes that only move people around or explain setup: the book does not yet work. A scene that turns but the writer cannot name the value: fixable.
- **Quote:** "It’s exposition, it’s fancy writing, it’s a lot of things, but it’s not a scene." (S001)
- **Also:** "A story has to turn, it has to move, the value has to shift." (S016)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S007, S008, S012, S016, S020)

## 3. Beginning hook, middle build, ending payoff (with Russian dolls)
- **What it is:** Every story, and every unit inside it, has three parts. The hook grabs the reader, the build ratchets up stakes and progressive complications, and the payoff resolves the hook. The same shape repeats at every size, like Russian dolls: beat, scene, sequence, act, subplot, global story. He applies the three-part split to any problem, including a nonfiction manuscript (S020).
- **Parts:** The three-sentence test: how does it start, what happens in the middle, how does it end (S003). The ending must mirror the inciting incident (S001) and be surprising and inevitable (S001, S003). Know the ending early; the work is making it surprising (S008).
- **When to use:** Before drafting (as the Foolscap), and on any manuscript where the writer cannot summarize the book.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the writer gives three plain sentences and the ending answers the question the hook raised. 3 if the hook and ending are clear but the middle is a list of events with no rising stakes. 1 if the writer needs a paragraph to say how it starts, or the ending resolves a different problem from the hook. Ask: is the ending surprising, and is it inevitable? Both must be yes.
- **Quote:** "The easier you can explain those three steps, the better your story is going to work." (S003)
- **Also:** "the ending pay-off has to mirror the inciting incident" (S001)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S003, S008, S012, S020, S023)

## 4. The five commandments of storytelling
- **What it is:** The five parts every unit of story needs: inciting incident, progressive complications (ending in a turning point), crisis, climax, resolution. They are the scene-level engine under the value turn.
- **Parts:**
  - *Inciting incident:* the first throw of the lead's life out of balance, relatively large, and hooking with something the reader half understands (S008, S016). It can be anticipated or unexpected, causal (another character) or coincidental (the environment) (S016). Ask what lie the character is telling himself (S016).
  - *Progressive complications:* small, rising irritants, each bigger than the last. The last one, the turning point, shifts the value (S016).
  - *Crisis:* a best bad choice, or two goods that cannot both be kept (S016, S006).
  - *Climax:* the choice made. *Resolution:* what follows, setting up the next unit (S016).
- **Changed over time:**
  - 2019 (S016): he put a golden rule above the five commandments (see entry 15, phere) and the power of 10 for scoring complications.
  - 2022, newer (S007): a finer taxonomy built with Danielle Kiowski. Causal inciting incidents come as a particle (a direct, targeted hit) or a wave function (the ripples felt by witnesses); coincidental ones emerge from the environment. Turning points are active (an overt move by the antagonist) or revelatory (a tell). The best turning point is revelatory to the protagonist and active by the antagonist. He calls part of this a working hypothesis. This taxonomy is not in the 2015 book.
- **When to use:** Diagnosing a scene that does not work, or building one.
- **How to score (guidance):** Check each scene for all five. Missing crisis (the lead never faces a real choice) is the most damaging gap. Rate complications with the power of 10 (S016): a run like 2, 2, 2, 7 is flat; lift it to rise steadily. A crisis between a good and a bad choice is weak; a best bad choice or two irreconcilable goods is strong.
- **Quote:** "So which is the best bad choice?" (S016)
- **Also:** "And this is the real test for a very good turning point." (S007)
- **Sources:** 5 (S006, S007, S008, S012, S016)

## 5. Conventions and obligatory scenes
- **What it is:** What fans of a genre expect. An obligatory scene is a scene the reader must see on the page (the hero at the mercy of the villain in a thriller; the lovers meet, break up and reunite, plus a proof of love, in a love story). A convention is an expected element that is not a scene (a cast role, a villain's speech, the hero against massive forces). Each genre has roughly 5 to 8 of each.
- **Parts:** The global genre's conventions and obligatory scenes must be on the page; a subgenre's can be reported (S002). A subgenre scene can be merged into a global scene for a double whammy, but only two or three times a book (S002). Innovate the convention, do not skip it: Alien reinvents the discovery-of-the-crime scene as a dinner (S008). For a writer who hates planning, write one obligatory scene of the genre, about 1,000 words, many times, because the first four or five ideas are clichés (S008). Nonfiction has its own conventions (entry 17).
- **When to use:** After genre is named. When readers say the book was fine but unsatisfying.
- **How to score (guidance):** List the genre's obligatory scenes and tick each one on the page. Any missing global obligatory scene is a serious fault; a reader of the genre will feel cheated. Score higher when each is delivered in a fresh way. Conventions are fuzzier, so weigh them less (S002).
- **Quote:** "if I’m reading a thriller and there's no hero at the mercy of the villain scene, I'm disappointed" (S002)
- **Also:** "at the very minimum, you must deliver the promise of the genre that you choose" (S002)
- **Evolving:** In 2017 he said he was still working out the lists: "I’m dedicating the rest of my life into really clarifying what I believe those conventions and obligatory scenes are." (S002)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S008, S012, S023)

## 6. The editor's six core questions
- **What it is:** The checklist he built as an acquiring editor who had to decide fast whether a manuscript works, whether it can be better, and whether he can fix it. He answers all six himself before he talks to a writer (S008). He compares them to a doctor's vital signs (S003) and says they always get him back on track (S023).
- **Parts:** 1. What is the genre? 2. What are its conventions and obligatory scenes? 3. What is the point of view and narrative device? 4. What are the objects of desire (want and need)? 5. What is the controlling idea or theme? 6. What are the beginning hook, middle build and ending payoff? Each has its own entry here.
- **When to use:** After a first draft, wearing the editor hat (he agrees they are mostly an editor's tool, S003). For any manuscript review.
- **How to score (guidance):** One point per question the writer can answer in a plain sentence and the pages back up. 6: the book likely works and the job is polish. 3 or 4: fixable with a structural pass. Under 3: go back to genre before touching sentences. A book works when it delivers the promise of its genre.
- **Quote:** "There are these six main questions that I need to understand and have answers to before I can have a proper dialogue with the writer." (S008)
- **Also:** "A book that works delivers on the promise that the writer is making to the audience." (S002)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S003, S008, S023)

## 7. The controlling idea
- **What it is:** The story's meaning stated as cause and effect, which he prefers to the vaguer word theme (he takes it from Robert McKee). It is the ground from which the whole story derives.
- **Parts:** Start from the genre's value and write: value triumphs (or prevails) when X. Start very simple and refine as the draft shows where the story begins and ends (S003). In big idea nonfiction it must be stated blatantly and early; in narrative nonfiction it is the north star and never stated outright (S023).
- **Changed over time:**
  - 2019 (S003): a one-line formula, for example a love story's love triumphs when the lovers embrace each other's flaws. You do not need it before drafting, but finding it early saves the pain between drafts one and seven.
  - 2022, newer (S006): at the universal (blue) level, the controlling idea is about patterns of behavior when two values collide, a double factor problem, such as when to tell the truth and when not to snitch. Stories give specific contexts for decisions under duress, against the contradictory slogans the culture teaches.
- **When to use:** When the ending feels arbitrary, or scenes do not seem to add up to anything.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if it fits one sentence in the value-triumphs-when form and the climax proves it. 3 if the writer offers a topic word (justice, family) instead of a cause. 1 if the climax would prove the opposite idea. Do not let a writer stall on it before a first draft.
- **Quote:** "it’s really the ground from which all of your story will derive" (S003)
- **Also:** "It’s a double factor problem. When do I snitch and when do I not snitch?" (S006)
- **Sources:** 4 (S003, S006, S008, S023)

## 8. Masterwork study (make the implicit explicit) *(unnamed as a method)*
- **What it is:** Writers learn story patterns implicitly from thousands of stories. The Story Grid tries to make them explicit so a writer learns faster. The method: pick the genre-defining book, read it many times page by page, mark what moved you, and the obligatory scenes show up. Start with one masterwork, not three (S002). He read Pride and Prejudice about 20 times for the love story.
- **Parts:** Read widely in the genre first (pattern finding needs the volume, S003); then one masterwork deeply; then compare your manuscript scene by scene.
- **When to use:** A writer who does not know the genre's conventions, or who asks friends outside the genre for feedback (he says learn genre instead, S012).
- **How to score (guidance):** Ask the writer to name the masterwork of their genre and its obligatory scenes. Cannot name one: the risk of a missing convention is high.
- **Quote:** "If you want to write a novel like Stephen King, you’d better read all of Stephen King." (S019)
- **Also:** "To make the implicit explicit is a difficult chore." (S003)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S003, S012, S019)

## 9. Scene analysis (the Story Grid spreadsheet, the graph, shoe leather, the scene logline) *(the tools as a group)*
- **What it is:** The working tools for judging scenes. The Story Grid spreadsheet is an 18-column sheet that takes each scene from start to finish and shows, if you are honest, what works; it teaches you to be the third-party reader (S020). The grid itself is a graph of scenes and big moments so a writer can see what is missing instead of reading a slew of notes (S019). A shoe leather scene only moves people from A to B or explains setup; cut it (S012). The scene logline test: boil each scene to one plain sentence, write four or five versions, and keep the one that shows each character's real role (S008).
- **When to use:** Line by line structural edit after the six questions; when the writer needs to see the problem rather than be told.
- **How to score (guidance):** Share of scenes with a clear value shift (entry 2); count of shoe leather scenes to cut; whether the big moments on the graph sit where the genre needs them. A diagnosable scene problem (it does not move, nothing is at stake, no value shifts, S020) is good news: it can be fixed.
- **Quote:** "That’s shoe leather. That’s what I call a shoe leather scene, Tim, meaning it’s unnecessary." (S012)
- **Also:** "The Story Grid takes the temperature of the health of the individual story." (S019)
- **Sources:** 4 (S008, S012, S019, S020)

## 10. Objects of desire (want and need)
- **What it is:** Two questions: what does the lead want, and what does the lead need. The want is external and set by the genre (in action, to survive). The need is internal, a step up Maslow's hierarchy, and is often granted by accident (S003).
- **Parts:** State the want in external terms; make it clear early and even have the lead voice it in early drafts (S003). In a scene, give two characters opposite wants, and test flipping who wants what (S012). A lead with a goal and a desire makes the reader root for them (S016). Adding an inner need can save a thin external story: Danny Boyle gave 127 Hours a need for self-transcendence (S003).
- **When to use:** When readers do not care about the lead, or the call to adventure comes late.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the want is stated externally in the opening act and the need is visible in the lead's inner conflict. 3 if only the want is clear (fine for some action stories). 1 if the reader cannot tell what the lead is after by the end of the hook.
- **Quote:** "the objects of desire are two questions; what does the character want and what does the character need?" (S003)
- **Also:** "whenever someone has a goal and a desire, we can’t help as human beings but root for them." (S016)
- **Sources:** 3 (S003, S012, S016)

## 11. Point of view and narrative device
- **What it is:** Who tells the story, to whom, and why. Once the storyteller is chosen, the story almost writes itself; Pressfield could not write The Legend of Bagger Vance until he found the old man telling a boy (S002).
- **Parts:** First person, third person, third omniscient, or free indirect style (S002, S012). Narrative altitude: open from 30,000 feet with a narrator or on the ground in dialogue, and ease the reader down (S012). In a first novel, one protagonist the reader can attach to (S012). A narrator telling the story to one listener has a reason for telling it; in 2022 he floated a double time signature for such narrators (the telling later and the scene now) as a hypothesis (S007).
- **When to use:** When the voice wobbles, the draft head-hops, or a writer is stuck before the first page.
- **How to score (guidance):** Red flag if point of view or device changes without reason (S002). Six viewpoints in a debut is a warning sign (S012). Strong if the narrator choice explains why this story is being told.
- **Quote:** "If they start changing the point of view or the narrative device without reason, then it’s a red flag to the editor" (S002)
- **Also:** "once they make the choice of who's telling the story, the story almost writes itself" (S002)
- **Sources:** 3 (S002, S007, S012)

## 12. Writer hat, editor hat
- **What it is:** Two jobs done at two times. Draft with no editing and no genre worry; the first draft's only job is to reach the end. Then put on a different hat and treat the editor as a separate person who brings out the Story Grid (S001, S012). He runs both in his own head.
- **When to use:** A writer revising chapter one forever, or a writer applying editorial notes during the idea stage (he advises against it, S012).
- **How to score (guidance):** Not a score for the manuscript but for the process. A finished rough draft beats a polished first act. Do not judge a first draft as a verdict on the writer (S012).
- **Quote:** "you have to wear two hats. You have to wear your writing hat and your editing hat." (S012)
- **Also:** "the goal for your first draft is get to the end" (S001)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S002, S012)

## 13. Define the problem, then break it down
- **What it is:** The hard work is defining the problem with specificity. Then break it into a hierarchy: one monster problem, six huge ones (the six questions), 15 lesser ones, then 70 or 80 (S003). Treat each like a faulty shower or a carpenter's bad right angle: list it, fix it, leave ego out (S019).
- **When to use:** A writer overwhelmed by a mess of a draft, or avoiding the work entirely.
- **How to score (guidance):** A manuscript note that names a specific, fixable problem scores; a note like the middle is slow does not (S008).
- **Quote:** "the very difficult work is in defining the problem" (S003)
- **Also:** "The way to beat anything is to break it down into a series of long tiny problems that you can beat down one by one, by one by one." (S020)
- **Sources:** 3 (S003, S019, S020)

## 14. The Foolscap Global Story Grid
- **What it is:** A one-page map of the whole book, inspired by Steven Pressfield, filled in before drafting and used as an editor's pointer. He built it because notes like the middle is slow did not help writers (S008).
- **Parts:** 2015 (S001): four major questions, the genre, then hook, build and payoff; do the inciting incident and the ending payoff together, then fill the middle. Late 2015 (S008): fuller boxes for external genre, core value, internal genre, controlling idea, point of view, inciting incident and the act turns. Minimum before drafting: the top quarter, external genre and core value. Leave fuzzy boxes fuzzy and fix them when the draft shows the problem (S008).
- **When to use:** Before a first draft (a map, like a road trip, not a detailed outline), and as the summary page for a revision.
- **How to score (guidance):** Can the writer fill the page? Genre and core value blank: stop and decide. The rest blank before drafting: acceptable.
- **Quote:** "there's only really four major questions that you have to answer before you can start working through your first draft" (S001)
- **Also:** "I think at the very least what you want to do is to get the top quarter of it done." (S008)
- **Sources:** 2 (S001, S008)

## 15. The golden rule: phere in every unit of story (2019, evolving)
- **What it is:** An idea he reached the night after a hard day at the 2019 Story Grid editor training, when he could not explain the inciting incident. Every unit of story, beat to global story, must have a phere (spoken like fear): fear or tension that forces a character to change behavior to reach a goal. The golden rule sits at the top; the five commandments sit under it as levels of phere.
- **Parts:** The inciting incident is a big, many-sided ball of phere; progressive complications are micro irritants that should grow; the turning point shifts the value. Vary the size: the same phere in every unit is boring. Power of 10: rate each complication's fear on a 10-point scale and check it rises.
- **Status:** New and still forming in April 2019. He said he had not been communicating it well. The 2022 sources (S006, S007) teach the five commandments with the causal and coincidental taxonomy instead and do not use the term, so treat phere as one stage of the method, not the settled form.
- **How to score (guidance):** If complications fail, zero in on phere: check size, complexity and variety. A flat run of fear levels scores low.
- **Quote:** "The Story Grid golden rule is thou must have phere in every unit of your story." (S016)
- **Also:** "If you have the same phere in every unit of story, it’s boring too." (S016)
- **Sources:** 1 (S016)

## 16. Yin-and-yanging the external and internal arcs (a cheat sheet)
- **What it is:** Do not let the lead win both the external and internal arcs, or lose both. Move them against each other: at the end of act one, nudge the external a little positive while the internal stays negative, then flip. Rocky loses the fight and wins his self-respect; Kramer vs. Kramer loses the external and wins the internal. This is how he finds the surprising half of surprising but inevitable.
- **When to use:** An ending that feels false or too neat.
- **How to score (guidance):** Both arcs end positive (or both negative): flag it as likely to ring false. Arcs in counterpoint: strong.
- **Quote:** "Here’s a cheat sheet. Don’t have both of them." (S008)
- **Sources:** 1 (S008)

## 17. Negation of the negation (the end of the line)
- **What it is:** Taking the genre's core value past its negative to the worst state: life, unconsciousness, death, damnation; justice, injustice, tyranny. Only for the global value, and only needed for a breakout. Harris takes Silence of the Lambs to the end of the line by the midpoint; Robert Crais broke out with LA Requiem when the fix took Elvis Cole to damnation.
- **When to use:** A competent genre book that is not breaking out.
- **How to score (guidance):** Global value reaches the negation of the negation: breakout potential. Stays at the plain negative: can still work well (Agatha Christie never went there). Scenes pushed to the extreme: a fault, it turns melodramatic.
- **Quote:** "to take the story to the very, very limits of human experience; to the end of the line" (S001)
- **Also:** "Agatha Christie never went to the negation of the negation." (S001)
- **Sources:** 1 (S001)

## 18. Big idea nonfiction conventions
- **What it is:** Nonfiction has genres too: academic (logos), how-to (ethos), narrative nonfiction (pathos), and big idea, which blends all three. Its conventions, modeled on Gladwell's The Tipping Point: persuasion by ethos, logos and pathos; the big idea stated blatantly early (about the first 500 words, then again); a destination promise; hero, victim and villain, with the villain named; sidekicks who explain parts of the program; the hero at the mercy of the villain; the Kubler-Ross change cycle as way stations, with depression as the turning point; and an ironic ending, good and bad at once.
- **When to use:** Memoir-driven or idea-driven books, and memoir where the writer's own change cycle carries the book.
- **How to score (guidance):** Tick each convention. A bare claim with no evidence fails logos. No named villain or no all-is-lost moment is a missing obligatory scene. Tim Grahl recalled a 10-80-10 hook, build and payoff split for big idea books (against 25-50-25 for fiction); Shawn did not restate it, so it is unverified.
- **Quote:** "So the big idea must be blatantly stated early on in the beginning hook of the story." (S023)
- **Also:** "This is the hero at the mercy of the villain. You’ve got to have it." (S023)
- **Sources:** 1 (S023)
