# Positions: Shawn Coyne

23 writer and editor topics, built from the batch notes on 12 sources (Creative Penn interview, Story Grid podcast episodes and posts, and the Starve the Doubts PDF, 2014 to 2022). Each stance has a verbatim quote, source id, and date (see `sources.md`). Most-used first, by number of sources. Where his view moved, the versions sit side by side with dates and the newer one is marked. Topics with no evidence say so. Points the notes flag as Tim Grahl's or unverified are left out or marked.

## The writer's mindset and Resistance
- **Stance:** Writing is blue collar craft, not a gift from the muse. The enemy is inside: self-sabotage, shame, and the stories you tell yourself. Love the labor, expect nothing back, and break the fear into small craft problems.
- "If I had to pinpoint the one thing that writers notoriously do, it's they self-sabotage." (S019, 2015-06)
- "There's a discipline to writing that's not very romantic. It's hard work." (S019, 2015-06)
- "The labor is really where you need to get your sustenance, not the results of the labor." (S019, 2015-06)
- "The big secret is you've got to love what you do. And you've got to work hard, and you can't expect anything in return." (S024, 2014-12)
- "that thing that you are avoiding the most is the obstacle that your insides are telling you to fight. So use it as your north star." (S020, 2016-12)
- "This is the thing we have to remember about the stories we tell ourselves; they're not true." (S020, 2016-12)
- "You need to attack the things that scare you the most because you just have to beat them down." (S008, 2015-11)
- "shame, it never produces great stuff" (S023, 2017-08)
- Success does not end it: "That's when resistance really bears down on you because it's like that old Peggy lee song, is that all there is?" (S020, 2016-12)
- He admits his own doubt: "it's the thing I struggle with every single day" (S024, 2014-12)
- **Note:** Resistance and turning pro are Steven Pressfield's ideas, and he credits them that way: "I'll just tell you what Steve Pressfield always says and he's always right about this" (S020, 2016-12)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S003, S008, S012, S019, S020, S023, S024)

## How to revise
- **Stance:** Define the problem first, break it into small named problems, and fix them one at a time. Take yourself out of it. The six core questions and the Story Grid are the diagnostic.
- "Just find your problems and make a list of your problems, go step-by-step and fix them." (S019, 2015-06)
- "They're not the problem. The problems are the problem." (S019, 2015-06)
- "the very difficult work is in defining the problem" (S003, 2019-02)
- "The way to beat anything is to break it down into a series of long tiny problems that you can beat down one by one, by one by one." (S020, 2016-12)
- A scene problem is fixable once named: "the problem is my scene doesn't move. There's nothing at stake in the scene, there's no value at stake" (S020, 2016-12)
- For flat complications, score them: "it's like what I call the power of 10 when you are analyzing your progressive complications" (S016, 2019-04)
- Fix the big intent before the lines: "I don't want to micro edit until you know, the whole thing is figured out." (S023, 2017-08)
- When a pro is stuck, look for the missing turn: "we discovered he missed a major shifting point in the middle build" (S001, 2015-02)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S003, S008, S012, S016, S019, S020, S023)

## Genre choice and mixing genres
- **Stance:** Genre is the first and best question. Pick one global genre, say it plainly, and deliver it; everything else is over-delivery. Labels like "literary fiction" are a way of hiding.
- "What's the genre is the best question you as a writer can ever, ever ask yourself, and you need to ask yourself all the time." (S002, 2017-12)
- "the big mistake that writers make is they don't embrace genre" (S001, 2015-02)
- "you have to pick one genre and nail it and then everything else over delivers to the audience" (S002, 2017-12)
- "always go with your global genre. Solve that puppy first." (S002, 2017-12)
- "It's just someone who's afraid to pick a genre." (S003, 2019-02), on writers who hide behind a literary label.
- "Make your choice. Put it down." (S008, 2015-11) Choose the external genre first, then the internal genre.
- **Mixing:** Allowed, with limits. Fold a subgenre scene into a global-genre scene for a "double whammy", but only "maybe two or three times in the novel" (S002, 2017-12). A subgenre's scenes can be reported: "As long as you deliver your global genre stuff, some of the other things can be reported." (S002, 2017-12) In a first scene, do not cross-pollinate; a crime layer over an action story should come late: "I think you can do that, but use it. Don't reveal it." (S012, 2015-11)
- **Read:** classification is fluid in his own hands (he reads Rocky as redemption but allows other readings; S008), and a genre can change in drafting as long as you check it each time (S002). Inferred from S002, S008.
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S002, S003, S008, S012, S019, S020)

## Beginnings
- **Stance:** Grab the reader at once, start in the middle of things, put the inciting incident in the first scene, and keep it simple: two people who want opposite things.
- "The beginning is supposed to really grab the reader by the throat and throw them into a vortex of alternate reality, so that they don't want to surface." (S003, 2019-02)
- "When you start a story, you want to begin it in the middle of things." (S012, 2015-11)
- "A major thing that first-time writers do is they over explain" (S012, 2015-11)
- "when it's two people, the reader doesn't have to work very hard to place who is who and what is what." (S012, 2015-11)
- "The inciting incident, chances are, the inciting incident is going to be relatively large." (S016, 2019-04)
- On a dramatic opener: "You need to make sure in your mind, if that's what you want to lead with, that this is really going to be the controlling force of the story." (S008, 2015-11)
- **Over time (the inciting incident):**
  - 2019-04: causal or coincidental: "It could be caused by another character or it could be a coincidental, chaotic environmental effect." (S016)
  - 2022-04 (newer): causal splits into particle and wave: "causal incitement has two flavors. One is particle, which means a direct targeted movement of energy from one to another, intentionally." (S007) He says this is newer than the 2015 book.
- **Nonfiction:** "So the big idea must be blatantly stated early on in the beginning hook of the story." (S023, 2017-08)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S003, S007, S008, S012, S016, S023)

## The global value and scenes that turn
- **Stance:** Every genre has a core value with gradations. Every scene must start at one charge and end at another. If nothing turns, it is not a scene.
- "the global value at stake for a crime novel is justice: are they going to catch the criminal or aren't they?" (S001, 2015-02)
- "A value means it has gradations. It has pulls. There's love and hate. There's truth and lies." (S008, 2015-11)
- "It's exposition, it's fancy writing, it's a lot of things, but it's not a scene." (S001, 2015-02)
- "A story has to turn, it has to move, the value has to shift." (S016, 2019-04)
- "The big takeaway from this episode is this: FOCUS ON SCENES!" (S012, 2015-11)
- Scenes can turn on other values: "the scene needs to have a value at stake" (S001, 2015-02), not always the global one.
- **Breakouts:** take the core value to the end of the line, past the negative (death to damnation), but never in single scenes: "if you do, it's going to seem melodramatic; it's going to seem like a soap opera" (S001, 2015-02). A breakout is optional: "Agatha Christie never went to the negation of the negation." (S001, 2015-02)
- **Over time:** 2019-04 (newer) he puts a "golden rule" above the five commandments: "The Story Grid golden rule is thou must have phere in every unit of your story." (S016) He says the term was still forming.
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S007, S008, S012, S016, S020)

## Editors
- **Stance:** An editor diagnoses and points; only the writer can fix the book. Big houses no longer give editors time, so writers must learn to edit themselves.
- "As an editor, I can tell them what the problems are, I can be a cheerleader, but I can't be the one to fix their book for them." (S019, 2015-06)
- "The trouble is finding the problems, and that's what editors used to do in book publishing" (S001, 2015-02)
- "I think editing is one of those really misunderstood skillsets" (S024, 2014-12)
- Vague notes are useless: "it will be much easier to fix their book than to just say things like, 'Well, the middle's a little slow.' That just doesn't help." (S008, 2015-11)
- "It takes just as much time to think through and diagnose a writer's problems as it does for the writer to bang out a first draft of something." (S012, 2015-11)
- Success weakens editing: "Once a writer reaches a certain level of commercial success, the publishing company that publishes that writer demands less and less of them editorially." (S019, 2015-06)
- Writer plus editor is the best pair: "When you have an implicit storyteller working with an explicit editor, what you combine is peanut butter and chocolate" (S003, 2019-02)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S003, S008, S012, S019, S024)

## Conventions and obligatory scenes
- **Stance:** Each genre has about 5 to 8 obligatory scenes and 5 to 8 conventions the reader expects. Deliver them on the page, and find them by reading the genre's masterwork many times. Innovate them; do not skip them.
- "A book that works delivers on the promise that the writer is making to the audience." (S002, 2017-12)
- "Conventions are things that aren't scenes that everybody expects in a genre." (S002, 2017-12)
- "if I'm reading a thriller and there's no hero at the mercy of the villain scene, I'm disappointed" (S002, 2017-12)
- "analyze the masterworks and you'll find them" (S002, 2017-12)
- "you have to be really, really, really well read." (S012, 2015-11)
- Innovate the scene: Alien turned the discovery of the crime into a dinner (S008, 2015-11).
- Nonfiction has them too: "This is the hero at the mercy of the villain. You've got to have it." (S023, 2017-08)
- **Limit:** "So conventions, yeah, I could say that could be a little bit fuzzy." (S002, 2017-12) He says the lists are still being refined.
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S002, S008, S012, S023)

## Marketing a book
- **Stance:** Find your core readers first and widen from there. Give content away. There is no launch date. Sell the global genre, not the subplot.
- "you really need to nail the core market for the book" (S024, 2014-12)
- "the way to sell more books is to create content and give it away for free to people who share the same passions that you do" (S024, 2014-12)
- "We were trying to coerce people to buy the book instead of having them discover the book and buy it themselves." (S024, 2014-12)
- "There's no such thing as a pub date, a launch date." (S001, 2015-02)
- "It's a big part of your book, but it's not what you're going to sell" (S002, 2017-12), on a subplot.
- The fair test: "if you don't get to 10,000 you will never know whether or not something can become a perennial bestseller." (S023, 2017-08)
- "Now, to be fighting to be on the best seller list is silly" (S020, 2016-12)
- **Limit:** his own 99-cent promotion "fell on its face" (S024).
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S002, S020, S023, S024)

## How long a book takes
- **Stance:** Years, and many drafts. No shortcut. Inferred from S001, S003, S008, S019.
- First novels: "it took you seven years to write it, and it's going to take another two years to publish it" (S001, 2015-02), offered as an example, not a rule.
- "it's the sorrow and pain in between draft 1 and 7 that can throw somebody off of the discipline" (S003, 2019-02)
- Even one scene idea: "it takes ten times to figure out something that's unique and different" (S008, 2015-11)
- "It's the Malcolm Gladwell 10,000 hour rule for writing just as it is for anything else." (S019, 2015-06)
- "A professional is patient, and they're not looking for overnight success" (S020, 2016-12)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S003, S008, S019, S020)

- **No recorded position** on agent or publisher rejection letters as such.

(S020, 2016-12) "They're not the problem. The problems are the problem." ## Feedback and beta readers
- **Stance:** Learn genre rather than poll friends who do not know it. Take notes, ask only to clarify, and let feedback settle before you react. The method's goal is to make you your own third-party reader.
- "I think it's better for you to spend your time learning more about genre than to ask people who don't know really the global concepts of genre to give you an opinion" (S012, 2015-11)
- "Don't explain or argue. Listen and give yourself time to metabolize before you react." (S012, 2015-11)
- Workshops without a method: "everybody rips it to shreds and makes you feel terrible without giving you any practical advice about how to make your work better." (S019, 2015-06)
- "it teaches you how to be that third party, non-family member who can look at your work in a constructive way." (S020, 2016-12)
- Get feedback early: "I'm doing my best to keep him from making catastrophic mistakes (the kinds that usually take 50,000 words to discover)." (S012, 2015-11)
- **Sources:** 4 (S008, S012, S019, S020)

## First drafts versus editing
- **Stance:** Two jobs, two hats. The first draft's only job is to reach the end; edit afterwards with the editor hat on. A bad draft is information, not a verdict.
- "the goal for your first draft is get to the end" (S001, 2015-02)
- "Don't begin your day by going over what you wrote yesterday." (S001, 2015-02)
- "you have to wear two hats. You have to wear your writing hat and your editing hat." (S012, 2015-11)
- "It's a great wild riff that's going to give you a lot of information for the future" (S012, 2015-11)
- "Don't kill yourself if you write a cheesy scene. So what? Nobody's going to read it." (S008, 2015-11)
- He agrees the six core questions are mostly for after a draft: "I would agree with that." (S003, 2019-02)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S003, S008, S012)

## Endings
- **Stance:** The ending must be surprising and inevitable, and it must mirror the inciting incident. Knowing it early is fine. Get the surprise by playing the outer and inner arcs against each other.
- "the ending of the story has to be inevitable but surprising" (S001, 2015-02)
- "the ending pay-off has to mirror the inciting incident" (S001, 2015-02)
- "There's no shame in knowing your ending at the beginning." (S008, 2015-11)
- "Here's a cheat sheet. Don't have both of them." (S008, 2015-11): do not give the lead both the external and the internal win.
- Nonfiction: "An ironic ending meaning it's good and it's bad." (S023, 2017-08) and "It ends with a paradox." (S023)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S003, S008, S023)

## Point of view and narrative device
- **Stance:** Who tells the story is a core choice; get it right and the book can write itself. In a first novel, one protagonist. Change point of view only for a reason.
- "once they make the choice of who's telling the story, the story almost writes itself" (S002, 2017-12)
- "If they start changing the point of view or the narrative device without reason, then it's a red flag to the editor" (S002, 2017-12)
- "the way people become emotionally attached to the story is to identify with one particular character." (S012, 2015-11)
- "six characters is a very, very difficult thing to pull off" (S012, 2015-11)
- "I would not recommend doing this, but if you were trying to write a society novel with multi-protagonists" (S003, 2019-02)
- First-person narrators who tell the story later: a working idea, by his own label: "here's my hypothesis, as it stands right now" (S007, 2022-04)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S003, S007, S012)

## Traditional versus self-publishing
- **Stance:** The Big Five can push a great book hard but chase a few brands. Independent publishing gives speed and control and suits a certain temperament. It is not for everyone.
- "they've got the ability to jam a book into the marketplace, and if it's great, it can become a huge bestseller" (S001, 2015-02)
- "So they take a smaller and smaller number of projects and try and make them big, big brands." (S024, 2014-12)
- "It was about feeding the monster, it was about getting the book into the bookstore. Good is good enough." (S020, 2016-12)
- "if you're independent, you can publish your first novel in September and publish your second novel in October" (S001, 2015-02)
- **Over time:**
  - Earlier: he says he once thought he could argue anyone into going independent: "I used to think that if I made the right arguments to people" (S001, 2015-02)
  - 2014-12 and 2015-02 (current view): "that's not appealing to everybody, but it is to a certain kind of temperament" (S024) and "It's not for everybody: if you like it, great; if you get nothing out of it, throw it away!" (S001)
- **Bias to weigh:** he left big houses, ran Rugged Land, and co-founded Black Irish Books (S019, S020, S024).
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S019, S020, S024)

## The controlling idea
- **Stance:** Use the controlling idea, not a vague theme. Start simple, sharpen it as the draft shows you, and do not freeze over it.
- **Over time:**
  - 2017-08: in nonfiction, short and plain: "I think what you want to do is you want to boil it down to this simple as possible phrase." (S023) In narrative nonfiction it guides but is not stated: "you use the controlling idea theme as your north star" (S023)
  - 2019-02: the formula, value triumphs when X: "Love triumphs when the two lovers recognize and embrace the flaws of their partner as much as the good things about them." (S003) "I prefer Robert McKee's concept of the controlling idea, because it's more specific" (S003)
  - 2022-04 (newer): at the deepest level it is about weighing two goods: "It's a double factor problem. When do I snitch and when do I not snitch?" (S006)
- Timing: "do you need to know it when you begin writing? No, I don't think so." (S003, 2019-02)
- **Sources:** 3 (S003, S006, S023)

## Money and advances
- **Stance:** A big advance looks better than it is once time is counted, and control over your own book's fate can be worth more. A small, steady seller can be profitable.
- "even say getting a $100,000 advance for your first novel sounds great, but it took you seven years to write it" (S001, 2015-02)
- "this really isn't that much fun if I can't even affect the success of my own work" (S024, 2014-12)
- "So the guy who sells five to six thousand copies of a particular book can be a profitable person." (S024, 2014-12)
- Selling direct: "you don't have to sell as many copies to make a living" (S001, 2015-02)
- No guarantees: "If you could tell somebody how to create something successfully over and over and over again, you would be King Midas, right?" (S023, 2017-08)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S023, S024)

## Writing a first novel
- **Stance:** Master one genre you love, read it deeply, keep the cast small and the plot simple, and tell it through one protagonist. Get feedback at the scene level before you write 50,000 words.
- "find one genre that you know you want to write in and then read it across the board" (S002, 2017-12)
- "If you want to write a novel like Stephen King, you'd better read all of Stephen King." (S019, 2015-06)
- Over-plotting: "they jam in so much plot." (S012, 2015-11)
- A puzzle with ten suspects on an island is a trap for a debut (S012, 2015-11, on Christie's And Then There Were None).
- "Anybody who applies themselves and does the amount of work necessary can be a very fine writer." (S019, 2015-06), though not necessarily an unforgettable one.
- **Sources:** 3 (S002, S012, S019)

## Memoir and nonfiction
- **Stance:** Nonfiction has genres, conventions and obligatory scenes too. Big idea books blend ethos, logos and pathos, state the idea early, name a villain, and end in paradox. A true story still needs a want and a need.
- "Now, the very first thing that has to be in a big idea book is persuasion and there are three ways to persuade somebody of your big idea." (S023, 2017-08)
- "So hero, victim, villain are those three major figures that have to be in an action story. They also need to be in a big idea book." (S023, 2017-08)
- "A big idea book results from self-examination." (S023, 2017-08)
- "what interests me as an editor is what compelled the writer to write the damn thing in the first place." (S023, 2017-08)
- From a true story to film: "Danny Boyle solved that problem by really taking the substance of the non-fiction story" (S003, 2019-02) and adding an inner need.
- **Memoir as such:** no direct recorded position. Nearest: in Tim Grahl's own-story book, the writer's change cycle carries the book (S023, paraphrase only).
- **Unverified:** a 10-80-10 split for big idea books against 25-50-25 for fiction is Tim Grahl's recollection of Shawn, not Shawn's words (S023). Do not cite it as his.
- **Sources:** 3 (S003, S023, S024)

## Agents
- **Stance:** Few novelists land one, and an agent is the route to a traditional contract.
- "there's probably 8% of people who write a novel are going to find an agent" (S001, 2015-02)
- He says an author needs an agent for a traditional deal (S024, 2014-12, paraphrase only).
- As an acquiring editor he asks pitching agents the genre first: "I know my genre that I am required to deliver to my bosses" (S002, 2017-12)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S002, S024)

## Outlining versus discovery
- **Stance:** Not a full outline, but a one-page map. At minimum, know the external genre and core value before drafting. For writers who hate planning, start with one obligatory scene and fill the map later.
- "before you begin writing your first draft, I think it's important to set out a map" (S001, 2015-02)
- "there's only really four major questions that you have to answer before you can start working through your first draft" (S001, 2015-02)
- "I think at the very least what you want to do is to get the top quarter of it done." (S008, 2015-11)
- "What I suggest is to pick an obligatory scene from the genre that you are really enamored with, that you really want to write." (S008, 2015-11)
- Warning: "You can really wrap your mind into a really large vortex of pain by being so analytical at the beginning." (S008, 2015-11)
- He designs for both: "One of my challenges is to think about people who don't like to plan so much" (S008, 2015-11)
- **Sources:** 2 (S001, S008)
