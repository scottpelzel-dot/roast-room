# Stories: Shawn Coyne

18 stories and cases he reuses, built from the batch notes on 12 sources (2014 to 2022), each with the lesson he draws. Most-used first. Source ids in parentheses. Stories told by Tim Grahl or other hosts are left out.

1. **Gates of Fire and the first grid.** While editing Steven Pressfield's Gates of Fire, Shawn said he would put his grid on the draft, and Steve asked what he meant. Years later the grid showed Steve what he had done without knowing it. Lesson: the method came out of real editing, and it makes a pro's instincts explicit (S001, S016, S019).
2. **Leaving corporate publishing.** He moved from Dell to St. Martin's to Doubleday, then quit in 2000 to start Rugged Land, then co-founded Black Irish Books with Pressfield. A trigger was a publisher refusing his self-funded postcard campaign for a Steelers book. He says of Rugged Land: "I was about ten years ahead of my time." (S019) Lesson: he wanted control of his own work's success, so weigh his pro-independent bias (S019, S020, S024).
3. **Silence of the Lambs as the model thriller.** "Thomas Harris takes the book to the end of the line by the mid-point of that novel" (S001), which is why the second half cannot be put down. He also uses Starling's job offer, likely the tenth version Harris tried, to show cutting "shoe leather", and Starling as the one protagonist a reader attaches to. Lesson: take the core value to the limit, and find the version of a scene that shows each character's real need (S001, S008, S012).
4. **Pressfield as a client.** Steve was stuck for years on The Profession until they found a missing shift in the middle build. Later, handed a controlling idea, Steve did not argue; he said he could tweak a few named scenes to hammer it home. Lesson: even pros lose the core principles on the book that stretches them, and pros use explicit tools (S001, S003).
5. **The War of Art from a drawer.** When Shawn left Doubleday he asked Pressfield to join his new house. Steve said no and handed him a Xeroxed manuscript, The Writer's Life, which became The War of Art. Lesson: a big idea can sit in a drawer until structure turns it into a book (S019, S020).
6. **The Legend of Bagger Vance.** Pressfield could not write it until he found the device of an old man telling the story to a boy. "He didn't figure out how to tell this story until he cracked the storyteller." (S002) Lesson: the narrator choice can unlock a whole book (S002).
7. **LA Requiem and Robert Crais.** The fix was to take Elvis Cole toward damnation and deepen the lead. Lesson: the breakout came from pushing the core value to the end of the line (S001).
8. **Rocky and Kramer vs. Kramer.** Rocky loses the fight and wins his self-respect; Kramer loses the external case and wins the internal one. If Kramer also landed the big job, it would ring false. Lesson: "Don't have both of them." (S008) Play the outer and inner arcs against each other to find the surprise (S008).
9. **Alien's chest-burster.** The discovery-of-the-crime scene was reinvented as a dinner on a blue-collar ship, so well that nobody sees it as an obligatory scene. Lesson: innovate the convention; do not skip it (S008).
10. **127 Hours.** Danny Boyle made a man trapped by his arm hold a feature by adding an inner need: visions of a future son. "It was it was the need that drove him to accomplish the want." (S003) Lesson: a thin external story, even a true one, needs an internal need (S003).
11. **Tim's scuba scene.** Tim Grahl's first scene had six speakers, two inciting incidents, and exposition in line one. Shawn praised the turn, then said cut to two people with opposite wants and save the captain's death for later. Lesson: simplify the cast, one want each, and catch big mistakes at 1,500 words, not 50,000 (S012).
12. **The Shining job interview.** Jack dreads not getting the job; the irony is that getting it destroys him. Lesson: build the inciting incident from what the lead fears and the lie he tells himself (S016).
13. **The boring dinner story.** A friend recounts polished shoes and a cooked steak, and nothing turns. "People who start telling you episodes in their life, where the value shift never turns are the most boring people in the world, right?" (S016) Lesson: no turn, no story (S016).
14. **The editor training epiphany.** At the 2019 Story Grid editor training he could not explain the inciting incident to smart critics, felt his own crisis, and came up with the phere idea that night. "I just was not communicating to the group." (S016) Lesson: pushback made the method better (S016).
15. **The 99-cent promotion.** He spread an ebook deal across friends' sites with no core market and sold about 300 copies. "It fell on its face." (S024) Lesson: find your people first, then widen (S024).
16. **The 10,000 reader rule and The Tipping Point.** From book-reader numbers and decades of sales data he says "a publisher's job is to get a book into the hands of 10,000 people." (S023) He holds up The Tipping Point as the big idea model: a quick hook, the idea in the first 500 words, and an ironic ending. Lesson: give a book a fair test, and a payoff can be both good and bad (S023).
17. **Margaret Atwood and the neurosurgeon.** A surgeon tells Atwood he will write a novel when he retires; she asks how she might become a neuroscientist. Lesson: writing is a craft you study, not a lark (S019).
18. **The worried author and the bestseller fax.** A woman with three major-press books told him she feared she was not worthy; he told her that was a lie. His own stomach knotted each Wednesday when the bestseller list came by fax, until he decided it was silly. Lesson: the stories we tell ourselves are not true, and shadow-career success is hollow (S020).
