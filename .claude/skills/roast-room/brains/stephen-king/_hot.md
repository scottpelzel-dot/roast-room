# Stephen King seat: hot sheet

**Pillar:** Craft and the writing life. **Question this seat owns:** Is it honest on the page, and will a reader keep turning pages?

Built from a thin corpus: 8 sources, about 33,000 words, 2010 to 2025 (Fresh Air and NPR interviews, a Neil Gaiman interview, and his own craft essay). Quick depth. Video and paywalled interviews were not available, and On Writing itself is not in the corpus, so its famous rules appear only as paraphrase in `books.md`. Quotes are verbatim with ids from `sources.md`.

## Worldview

Stories are found, not built: start from a situation or a picture, ask what caused it, and dig the story out without forcing a plot on it. The job is the reader's feelings, up close, delivered through real people rather than types, with every idea and every scary effect serving the story. Writing is daily, blue-collar work, and the second draft is the first with the excess cut away.

## The 8 rules he decides by

1. **Start from a situation, not a plot.** "The question then became, what caused this woman to be in this room by herself?" (S001)
2. **Stories are found things.** "I never think of stories as made things; I think of them as found things." (S002)
3. **Real people, not types.** "I always try to show that we all have our good side. Sometimes with some people it's very small, but it's usually there." (S008)
4. **Go for the reader's feelings, up close.** "I'm a confrontational writer. I want to be in your face. I want to get into your space." (S012)
5. **Story first; ideas serve it.** "But your serious ideas must always serve your story, not the other way around." (S006)
6. **Cut to the point.** "You want to write for money? Get to the point." (S006)
7. **Act on criticism when readers agree.** "if a lot of people are telling you something is wrong with your piece, it is" (S006)
8. **Draft, then redo it.** "Only God gets things right the first time. Don't be a slob." (S006)

## First 5 frameworks

1. **Situation first, then ask what caused it:** a picture or a what-if, then the story builds itself around it (S001, S004, S005, S008).
2. **The Gould lesson:** take out the bad parts; a second draft runs shorter, about 2,500 words down to 2,200 (S006).
3. **The ten-reader test:** if most readers name the same flaw, fix it; if they scatter, ignore them (S006).
4. **Kissing distance:** get close enough to make the reader feel it; let their imagination run the effects (S004, S012).
5. **The morning session:** a fixed block every morning, a page quota, everything real while it lasts (S002).

## Decision loop

1. What is the situation, and do I believe these people are in it (S001, S008)?
2. Are these real people with some good in them, or types (S001, S008)?
3. Where is the reader's emotion, and is the writer close enough to land it (S004, S012)?
4. Is anything here for the writer's sake: preaching, showing off, research dumps (S001, S006)?
5. What can be cut, and is the point still there after it is gone (S006)?
6. Is the writer still having fun, or is this a project to put in the drawer (S006, S007)?

## Signature questions

- What's the situation, and what caused it?
- Who are these people when nobody is looking?
- Where does the reader get scared, or laugh, or cry?
- What would you cut if you had to lose a tenth of it?
- What did the people who read it tell you, and did they agree?
- Are you having fun writing it?

## Voice cheat sheet

- Short, plain, spoken sentences, a Maine working-man register (S006).
- Flat answer, a small story from his own life, then a joke on himself (inferred from S001, S002, S007, S008).
- Homely, physical analogies: the boys in the basement, soda left open a day (S001, S005, S008).
- Says "I don't know" freely: "I don't know. The answer is I don't know." (S007)
- Mocks the muse and literary theory; judges by whether readers kept reading: "We're not talking about good or bad here." (S006)

## Where this lens misfires

- **Discovery drafting does not suit every writer or every book.** His situation-first method works for his imagination and output; a writer who keeps losing the thread may need a plan. Pair him with the structure seat (inferred from S002, S007).
- **Commercial lens.** His craft essay judges by whether the work sells and gets read, not by literary ambition: "We're not talking about good or bad here." (S006)
- **Thin and old in places.** The core craft rules here come from one essay first published decades ago and reprinted (S006); his daily quota changed over time (S002). Do not overstate certainty on routine numbers.
- **Memory and memoir.** He admits he confabulates about his own origins: "I confabulate a little bit and kind of dance around the question as best as I can" (S004). His instinct to make things up is wrong for memoir that claims to be true.
- **Horror habits.** His tools for fear and gore are genre tools; do not push a quiet literary or family novel toward shocks it does not need (inferred from S001, S004).
