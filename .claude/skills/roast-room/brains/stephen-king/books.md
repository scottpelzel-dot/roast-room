# Books: Stephen King

Built from a thin corpus: 8 sources, about 33,000 words; video and paywalled interviews were unavailable and no book text was fetched. Summaries are in my own words. On Writing is his one craft book and gets the full entry. The novels follow as craft case studies, limited to what he says about how they were made in these sources. Years are first publication.

## On Writing: A Memoir of the Craft (2000; 10th anniversary edition 2010, 20th anniversary edition 2020)
- **Core thesis:** Writing well is a craft you can learn by reading a lot and writing a lot, with honest work and no tricks. The book is part memoir (his childhood, early jobs, rejections, drinking and recovery), part toolkit, and ends with the 1999 accident that nearly killed him and his return to work.
- **Key frameworks, in my own words (from the book; this corpus does not teach them, so no quotes):**
  - The toolbox: vocabulary, grammar and the basics of style sit on the top shelf; use the plain word you have, and cut adverbs and timid passive phrasing.
  - Read a lot and write a lot: there is no shortcut, and a writer who does not read has no tools.
  - Situation over plot: put people in a predicament and watch them work free, rather than steering them through an outline. The corpus shows him doing this (S001, S004, S008).
  - Door closed, door open: write the first draft alone and fast; open the door for the rewrite and the readers.
  - The Ideal Reader: write for one person whose judgment you trust (for him, his wife Tabitha) and imagine their reaction while revising.
  - Rest the draft before revising, then cut: the second draft should be shorter than the first. The corpus has his own cutting numbers from the Gould lesson (S006).
  - A daily quota: the book sets a higher daily word target than he reported in 2012 (six pages, about 1,200 to 1,500 words; S002). Treat the 2012 figure as newer.
  - Honesty in dialogue and character: people on the page should talk and act as they would, not as the writer would like.
  - The boys in the basement: the muse as working men in the subconscious, not a fairy. This is the one book framework quoted in the corpus (S001, S005).
- **Taught in sources:** S001 (2000 interview recorded after the accident, and the 2010 anniversary edition; he reads from the accident chapter and names the boys in the basement), S005 (replays the 2000 interview), S006 (points readers to the 20th anniversary edition). None of the sources walks through the toolbox, the Ideal Reader or door closed and open, so the seat should name those as the book's teaching and not quote them.

## How Stephen King Learned to Write, with the list "Everything You Need to Know About Writing Successfully" (essay; reprinted in The Writer's Survival Guide, 2005; posted by The Writer, 2025)
- **Core thesis:** A paid career in fiction comes down to a short list of hard rules, learned in ten minutes from an editor's black pen.
- **Key frameworks:** the Gould lesson (cut the bad parts; second drafts shorter); the twelve points; the ten-reader test for criticism; the quit ladder based on rejection counts; talent defined, for a beginner, as getting paid. See frameworks.md, 8, 9, 12 and 13.
- **Taught in sources:** S006 (the full text). The original publication date is not in the corpus.

## Danse Macabre (1981)
- **Core thesis:** His nonfiction book on horror in books, film and radio.
- **Taught in sources:** none in King's own words. Jordan Peele credits it with a distinction between terror and horror (S004), and Neil Gaiman cites it for page-a-day advice (S002). Both are their accounts, not King's, so the seat should not quote or lean on it.

## Carrie (1974)
- **What it shows:** Nothing in King's own words here. Gaiman reports that King threw it away and Tabitha pulled it out of the bin and pushed him to finish (S002). Use only as Gaiman's account.

## The Shining (1977) and Doctor Sleep (2013)
- **What they show:** An early idea can wait and turn into another book: The Shining first began as a family looking after an amusement park (S004). Doctor Sleep came from readers asking what happened to the boy, and from following Danny Torrance forward step by step into drink and a hospice job. He took the sequel because it was hard and gave him something to push against, and found the older girl harder to write than young Danny because he no longer had a child of that age at home.
- **Taught in sources:** S002, S004, S008, S012.

## Cujo (1981) and Gerald's Game (1992)
- **What they show:** Situation first. Cujo was mostly two people in a small space; Gerald's Game took that further to one woman alone in a room, and the cause of her being there came second, then a little research, then the subplot.
- **Taught in sources:** S001, S004.

## Different Seasons (1982)
- **What it shows:** Listen to the editor when he names what the book lacks. He sent three novellas; his editor pointed out that seasons come in four, so he wrote a fourth. It drew the best reviews of his life and loosened the horror label.
- **Taught in sources:** S002.

## Pet Sematary (1983) and It (1986)
- **What they show:** Write the thing that will not leave you, even if you think it is too awful to publish. He shelved Pet Sematary, published it only because of a contract, and it sold. It shows the same thing about a dark premise.
- **Taught in sources:** S007.

## The Green Mile (1996)
- **What it shows:** A format can free a stuck idea. A story that was struggling found its shape as a Dickens-style serial; he locked himself in, stayed ahead of the schedule, and found each piece he needed as he reached it.
- **Taught in sources:** S002.

## Bag of Bones (1998) and Misery (1987)
- **What they show:** Ideas come from gossip and from the writing life itself (a novelist who stockpiles books came from a story about another author). Both drew the horror label no matter what he wrote. He also notes that the nurses who cared for him after the accident had all read Misery.
- **Taught in sources:** S001, S002.

## Dreamcatcher (2001)
- **What it shows:** Using your own injury in fiction: he gave a character his broken leg and hospital stay, and kept other material back for later.
- **Taught in sources:** S001, S005.

## The Wind Through the Keyhole (2012) and the Dark Tower series
- **What they show:** Little in King's own words; S002 names the new book (as "The Wind in the Keyhole"). Gaiman reports King wondering whether to rewrite the series as one long novel. That is Gaiman's account.
- **Taught in sources:** S002.

## Joyland (2013)
- **What it shows:** Image first: a boy in a wheelchair flying a kite sat in his head for about 20 years before a story built around it. He picked a model park online, invented the rest, and let the setting drift toward the carnivals he remembered because he liked it better. Written for Hard Case Crime out of love for the pulp he read as a boy.
- **Taught in sources:** S004, S005, S008, S012.

## Revival (2014)
- **What it shows:** A character he wanted to write for years, drawn from the revival and TV preachers he watched for their rhythm of speech. A character who never comes onstage stays unsympathetic.
- **Taught in sources:** S008.

## You Like It Darker (2024) and "The Answer Man"
- **What they show:** Leave the unfinished story in the drawer. He began "The Answer Man" at about 30, lost it, and finished it at 75 in three acts across one man's life, writing the old man from experience.
- **Taught in sources:** S007.
