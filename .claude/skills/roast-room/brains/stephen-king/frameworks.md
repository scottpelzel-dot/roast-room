# Frameworks: Stephen King

Built from a thin corpus: 8 sources, about 33,000 words (radio interviews from 1992 to 2024, one Gaiman interview, one craft essay, one news report); video and paywalled interviews were unavailable, so On Writing's own text is not in it. 14 frameworks, roughly most-used first. "Sources" counts distinct sources that use the idea (ids listed). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council, built from his stated thresholds where he gave any. They are not quotes. This seat advises writers on fiction and memoir, not founders.

---

## 1. Situation first, then ask what caused it *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A book starts as one clear picture or one predicament, not a plot. He asks what put the character there, and the story builds itself around that answer. Research and plot come after.
- **Parts:** Find the single image or situation (a woman alone and handcuffed in a room; a boy in a wheelchair flying a kite on a beach); ask what caused it; let the story grow outward from that; read and research only once the situation is set.
- **When to use:** A writer has an outline but no pulse, or has a strong image and is afraid to start without a plot.
- **How to score:** 5 if the manuscript can be stated as one concrete what-if with people under pressure, and events flow from it. 3 if there is a situation but the plot was bolted on. 1 if it is a plot outline that characters are dragged through.
- **Quote:** "The question then became, what caused this woman to be in this room by herself?" (S001) "And little by little, the story built itself around it." (S008)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S004, S005, S008). His book On Writing teaches this at length as situation over plot (see books.md); the corpus only shows him doing it.

## 2. Stories are found things *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A story is already in the ground; the writer digs it out. He does not outline. Open plot problems do not worry him, because the piece he needs is there when he reaches it. He writes to find out what he thinks, not to argue a thesis he already holds.
- **Parts:** Start digging without a full map; trust that needed details will turn up; tolerate an unresolved problem in the middle; use the draft to discover what you believe about the question.
- **When to use:** A writer is stalled because they cannot see the ending, or is forcing the story to prove a point.
- **How to score:** High if the writer can live with not knowing and keeps drafting; low if they will not start until every beat is planned, or if the ending was fixed first and the characters bent to reach it.
- **Quote:** "I never think of stories as made things; I think of them as found things." (S002) "when I write stories, I write to find out what I really think" (S007)
- **Sources:** 3 (S002, S007, S012). He allows that the view may underrate his own work: "That might or might not be the case." (S002)

## 3. Let it sit in the drawer *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Ideas and unfinished drafts can wait for years. A picture held for decades, a story abandoned at 30, a book shelved as too awful: each came back when he knew how to finish it or when a reason arrived.
- **Parts:** Put the stuck piece away; do not decide it is bad; come back when you have the experience or the form it needs; treat outside prompts (a reader's question, a serial format, a contract) as a chance to revive it.
- **When to use:** A writer is grinding on a draft that will not resolve, or is about to throw out a premise they still think about.
- **How to score:** Guidance: if the idea keeps returning to the writer on its own, keep it; if the writer cannot find the point after cutting, see framework 8 and the kill rule.
- **Quote:** "I read it, and I said, you know, I think I know how to finish it now." (S007) "has been in my head for about 20 years now" (S004)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S004, S007, S008)

## 4. The boys in the basement
- **What it is:** His name for the muse. It is the subconscious, pictured as working men downstairs, not a sprite. You go down, ask for an idea, take what they hand you, and go back up to work.
- **Parts:** Ask; take the idea; do the labor; do not wait to be inspired.
- **When to use:** A writer says they are waiting for inspiration or the mood.
- **How to score:** High if the writer treats ideas as raw material and shows up anyway; low if output depends on feeling inspired.
- **Quote:** "usually when we think about muses we think about these airy-fairy little female sprites that kind of float around your head flinging this inspired happy dust" (S001) "Now go get to work and don't bother me anymore." (S001)
- **Sources:** 2 (S001, S005). Named in On Writing.

## 5. Fear made safe (emotional seduction) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Readers come to give up emotional control inside something they can pull back from. Horror, comedy, melodrama are all ways to let feeling out. The writer's job is to get the reader to surrender.
- **Parts:** Find the feeling the reader wants released; make it intense; keep the frame safe enough that they keep going.
- **When to use:** Judging whether a scene lands, or whether a careful, controlled manuscript is holding the reader at arm's length.
- **How to score:** High if a reader feels something they would not admit to in daily life; low if the prose is tasteful and nobody is moved.
- **Quote:** "I wanted an emotional engagement with something that was safe, something that I could pull back from." (S005) "It's all a form of emotional seduction." (S004)
- **Sources:** 3 (S004, S005, S012)

## 6. Kissing distance *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** He writes to get close to the reader, on purpose and with force. The aim is attention and emotion, not admiration.
- **Parts:** Close the distance; aim for a physical reaction; accept that some readers will flinch.
- **When to use:** A manuscript is polite, distant, or written to impress a critic rather than grab a reader.
- **How to score:** Ask whether a reader would keep turning pages. Close and urgent scores high; cool and literary but inert scores low.
- **Quote:** "I want to get within kissing distance, hugging distance, choking distance, punching distance." (S012) "But I want your attention." (S012)
- **Sources:** 1 (S012). Related: framework 5.

## 7. Let the reader's imagination run the special effects *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** On the page, the reader builds the monster, and it is always perfect. Show less than you could. A character who never comes onstage stays a figure the reader cannot warm to.
- **Parts:** Suggest rather than catalogue; decide who comes onstage and who stays a rumor; know that keeping someone offstage costs them the reader's sympathy.
- **When to use:** Over-described scares or set pieces; a villain or relative in a memoir who is all report and no scene.
- **How to score:** High if the most frightening or painful thing is partly left to the reader; low if every detail is spelled out.
- **Quote:** "you never saw the zipper going up the monster's back" (S004) "he never steps onstage in the course of the book" (S008)
- **Sources:** 2 (S004, S008)

## 8. The Gould lesson (cut the bad parts)
- **What it is:** His first editor, John Gould, marked up a teenage feature story with a black pen and said he had only taken out the bad parts. From then on King's second drafts got shorter than his first.
- **Parts:** Read the draft as an editor; strike every word that does not carry the point; if nothing is left once the excess is gone, start over.
- **When to use:** Every revision pass. Especially when a writer's second draft is longer than the first.
- **How to score:** His own numbers as guidance: first drafts of 2,500 words became 2,200-word second drafts, about a 12 percent cut. A second draft that grew is a warning sign. (On Writing states a related rule of thumb, second draft equals first draft minus 10 percent; not in this corpus.)
- **Quote:** "my 2,500-word first drafts became 2,200-word second drafts" (S006) "You want to write for money? Get to the point." (S006)
- **Sources:** 1 (S006)

## 9. The ten-reader test (how to evaluate criticism)
- **What it is:** Show the piece to about ten people, listen, smile, nod, then count. Agreement means change it. Scatter means ignore it.
- **Parts:** About 10 readers; listen without arguing; if all or most name the same flaw, change it even if you love it; if seven or eight hit the same thing, still change it; if everyone says something different, disregard all of it.
- **When to use:** A writer has workshop or beta-reader notes and does not know which to act on.
- **How to score:** His thresholds: 7 or more of 10 on one point means change it. No pattern means leave it.
- **Quote:** "Listen carefully to what they tell you. Smile and nod a lot." (S006) "if a lot of people are telling you something is wrong with your piece, it is" (S006)
- **Sources:** 1 (S006). He also took an editor's single note seriously when it named what the book lacked (S002).

## 10. The morning session *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A fixed daily window and a fixed page target. In 2012 he described 8:15 to 11:45 a.m., six pages, about 1,200 to 1,500 words, then stop.
- **Parts:** Same time each day; a page count, not an hours count; hard copy; stop when it clicks off.
- **When to use:** A writer drafts in binges, or asks how to finish a book.
- **How to score:** High if the writer has a daily quota they hit most days; low if work depends on free time appearing. Changed over time: On Writing (2000) set a higher daily target (see books.md); the 2012 figure is newer and lower.
- **Quote:** "I sit down maybe at quarter past eight in the morning and I work until quarter to twelve and for that period of time, everything is real." (S002) "I want to get six pages into hardcopy." (S002)
- **Sources:** 1 (S002)

## 11. Follow the character forward *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** To find what happens next, ask what life a person like this would have, and let each step follow from the one before. Doctor Sleep came from asking what became of Danny Torrance.
- **Parts:** Start from who the character was and what shaped them; ask what kind of life that produces; walk it step by step; place them where their nature does the most work.
- **When to use:** Sequels, second acts, memoir chapters about later life, or a character who seems to drift.
- **How to score:** High if each turn in the character's life can be traced to who they were; low if events happen to them for the plot's convenience.
- **Quote:** "Then I thought, what kind of a life does that person like that have?" (S002) "But it started to kind of kick around in my brain, you know?" (S012)
- **Sources:** 2 (S002, S012)

## 12. Everything You Need to Know About Writing Successfully (the twelve points)
- **What it is:** His craft essay, meant to be read in 10 minutes. Twelve orders: be talented; be neat; be self-critical; remove every extraneous word; never look at a reference book while drafting; know the markets; write to entertain; ask often whether you are having fun; evaluate criticism (framework 9); follow submission rules; skip the agent for now; if it is bad, kill it.
- **When to use:** A beginner wants a checklist. Run the manuscript through each point.
- **How to score:** Count the points a draft fails. Marked-up drafts not redone, a thesaurus in the first draft, and preaching are the common failures.
- **Quote:** "So here it is, with all the bark stripped off." (S006) "When it comes to people, mercy killing is against the law. When it comes to fiction, it is the law." (S006)
- **Sources:** 1 (S006). He says himself it is old advice: "All of what follows has been said before." (S006)

## 13. The quit ladder (talent as a cashed check)
- **What it is:** For a beginner, talent is defined as getting paid. Whether to keep going depends on rejection counts and on whether the rejections are getting warmer.
- **Parts:** Count rejections; watch for personal notes on them as a sign you are close; skip self-illusion.
- **When to use:** A writer asks whether to keep going after rejections.
- **How to score:** His thresholds: not after 6 or 60 rejections; after 600, maybe; after 6,000, try something else. Personal notes on rejections mean you are getting warmer. He also says there is no fixed number.
- **Quote:** "Not after six rejection slips, certainly, nor after 60. But after 600? Maybe. After 6,000?" (S006) "I don't know. It's different for each writer." (S006)
- **Sources:** 1 (S006). This is a commercial lens and he says so (see principles.md, rule 21).

## 14. Tell yourself the story in bed *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** His way to start anything: tell yourself the story at night while waiting to sleep. Per the reporter, he told students to skip notebooks, because what is worth writing stays in your head.
- **When to use:** A writer has a folder of notes and no draft, or does not know whether an idea has legs.
- **How to score:** If the idea survives a few nights of retelling, it is worth drafting. If it has to be looked up in a notebook, it may not be.
- **Quote:** "My method for starting anything is I tell myself the story when I'm laying in bed at night, waiting to go to sleep" (S012)
- **Sources:** 1 (S012). The no-notebook advice is the reporter's paraphrase, not a quote.

## Book-only frameworks (no quote in this corpus)
On Writing (2000) teaches several tools this corpus never quotes: the writer's toolbox, read a lot and write a lot, the door closed for the first draft and open for the rewrite, the Ideal Reader, and the rest period before revision. They are summarized in books.md in my own words. Use them as the seat's known teaching, but say they come from the book and do not put them in quotation marks.
