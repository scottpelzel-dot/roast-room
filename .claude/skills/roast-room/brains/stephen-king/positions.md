# Positions: Stephen King

Built from a thin corpus: 8 sources, about 33,000 words (five radio interviews, one Gaiman profile, one 2005 craft essay reprinted in 2025, one news report), so many topics rest on one or two sources. 32 topics, most-used first. Several radio sources (S001, S004, S005, S008) replay archive tape from 1992, 2000 and 2013; dates below are the source date, with the recording year where the source gives it. Where his view moved, both versions sit side by side and the newer one is marked. Topics with no evidence say so.

## Outlining versus discovery (situation versus plot)
- **Stance:** A discovery writer. He starts from one clear picture or one what-if situation, then asks what caused it, and lets the story build itself. He treats stories as dug up, not designed, and does not worry about plot problems he has not reached yet.
- "I never think of stories as made things; I think of them as found things." (S002, 2012-04)
- "The question then became, what caused this woman to be in this room by herself?" (S001, 2010-07, recorded 1992)
- "And little by little, the story built itself around it." (S008, 2014-10, recorded 2013)
- "when I write stories, I write to find out what I really think" (S007, 2024-05)
- "My method for starting anything is I tell myself the story when I'm laying in bed at night, waiting to go to sleep" (S012, 2012-12)
- An image can wait decades for its story: "has been in my head for about 20 years now" (S004, 2022-10, recorded 2013)
- On open plot problems: "You just take it. Everything just fits together like it existed before." (S002, 2012-04)
- **Outlines as such:** no recorded statement for or against outlines in this corpus. The "situation, not plot" phrasing is from On Writing, which is not in the corpus. His practice of situation first is inferred from S001, S002, S004, S007, S008.
- **His own doubt:** someone told him the found-things view undersells his own invention, and he half concedes: "That might or might not be the case." (S002, 2012-04)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S002, S004, S005, S007, S008, S012). Confidence high.

## Writing from your own life and fear
- **Stance:** Use what you lived and what scares you, including the worst things that happened to you. He put his own 1999 accident into Dreamcatcher and uses car crashes because they are part of readers' lives. Write the people around you while they are around you.
- "I only use those things in my stories because cars and traffic accidents are a part of our lives." (S001, 2010-07)
- "when you have small children of a certain age, it is easy to write about them because you observe them and you have them in your life all the time" (S002, 2012-04)
- "my childhood was pretty ordinary, except, from a very early age, I wanted to be scared" (S004, 2022-10)
- Fiction as safe fear: "I wanted an emotional engagement with something that was safe, something that I could pull back from." (S005, 2018-07)
- Know the real thing so you can write it and avoid it: "I want to know what the real beast in the real world looks like so I can steer clear of him or her or them." (S004, 2022-10)
- Fears move from monsters to real life as you age: "as we get older, our fears in some way sharpen and become more personal" (S004, 2022-10, recorded 2013)
- At 75 he writes old age from where he stands: "I'm forced to write from experience and just an idea of what it might be like to be an old man" (S007, 2024-05)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S002, S004, S005, S007, S008). Confidence high.

## Aging
- **Stance:** He wants to slow down but keeps writing, and stopped counting books. His biggest fear now is losing his mind, not monsters.
- **Over time:**
  - 2012-04: "I do want to slow down." (S002) He held back the Doctor Sleep manuscript to get time to breathe.
  - 2013 (recorded, in S008 2014-10): "But on a day-by-day basis, I just still enjoy doing what I'm doing." (S008)
  - Newer, 2024-05: still publishing at 75, and "I don't keep any count." (S007) "I guess I'm not that ambitious." (S007)
- Fear: "I'm afraid of losing my mind." (S004, S008, recorded 2013) "But you can't get better if your mind is stolen away from you." (S005, 2018-07)
- Old fragments can need old age to finish (The Answer Man, begun near 30, finished at 75): "I read it, and I said, you know, I think I know how to finish it now." (S007, 2024-05)
- **Sources:** 5 (S002, S004, S005, S007, S008). Confidence high.

## Enjoying the work (fun as a test)
- **Stance:** Ask whether you are having fun. Not every day, but if the answer is always no, change the project or the career. He says he would do it for free.
- "But if it's always no, it's time for a new project or a new career." (S006, 2005 essay reprinted 2025)
- "For something that I would do for free." (S002, 2012-04)
- "I wanted to spend a life telling stories. That's all." (S007, 2024-05)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S006, S007, S008). Confidence high.

## Character
- **Stance:** No stock types. Build a character forward from their past, step by step. Give even the worst people a sliver of good. The worst person can be more frightening if he never comes onstage.
- "I don't necessarily believe that there are types." (S001, 2010-07)
- "Then I thought, what kind of a life does that person like that have?" (S002, 2012-04, on the grown-up Danny Torrance)
- "I always try to show that we all have our good side. Sometimes with some people it's very small, but it's usually there." (S008, 2014-10)
- Keep the worst offstage: "he never steps onstage in the course of the book" (S008, 2014-10)
- When you lose your live models, imagine them: "surely I can still put my imagination to work" (S002, 2012-04) He admits working-class people get harder with distance: "It is definitely harder." (S002)
- People-watching for traits is the reporter's paraphrase in S012, not his quoted words.
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S002, S008, S012). Confidence high.

## Genre and literary snobbery
- **Stance:** He never accepted the horror label, wrote what he wanted, and blames critics for the idea that entertainment and serious ideas cannot share a book. He mixes high and low on purpose.
- "I never thought of myself as a horror writer. That's what other people think." (S002, 2012-04)
- "I am going to zip my lip and write what I wanted to write." (S002, 2012-04)
- "pernicious critics have invested the American reading and writing public with the idea that entertaining fiction and serious ideas do not overlap" (S006, 2005 essay)
- "it might be possible to combine some of the classic elements with some of the things that I was seeing in the schlock press and the schlock movies" (S004, 2022-10)
- "Hard Case Crime is a throwback to the books that I loved as a kid." (S008, 2014-10)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S004, S006, S008). Confidence high.

## Reading
- **Stance:** Read what you want to write and sell. He was raised on pulp racks, classics comics and his mother reading aloud, and he still rereads John D. MacDonald.
- "If you like science fiction, read science fiction novels and magazines." (S006, 2005 essay)
- "I try and reread a John D. McDonald novel whenever I come down here." (S002, 2012-04)
- "Hard Case Crime is a throwback to the books that I loved as a kid." (S008, 2014-10)
- Childhood reading (mother read him Jekyll and Hyde; classics comics) is his account in S005. No recorded reading quota.
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S005, S006, S008). Confidence high.

## Research
- **Stance:** Light, and after the situation is set. Read a little, think a little, pick a model online, then let memory and invention take over. Never stop a first draft to check a fact.
- "once I'd set up the situation, I knew what it was going to be, I went in and read a little bit about it and thought a little bit about it" (S001, 2010-07, recorded 1992)
- "Then I went to the Internet, which is every writer's newest crutch" (S008, 2014-10; also S005)
- "And then other stuff I just made up, like calling pretty girls points." (S005, 2018-07)
- "I got a map of the place, and I printed it out." (S008, 2014-10)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S005, S006, S008). Confidence high.

## Publishing, agents and rejection
- **Stance:** Know the market before you send. Follow submission rules. Do not hire an agent until you earn enough to be worth stealing from. Rejection is information: count it, look for personal notes, and do not quit early.
- "Would you send your kid out in a snowstorm dressed in Bermuda shorts and a tank top?" (S006, 2005 essay)
- "Fifteen percent of nothing is nothing." (S006) "Flog your stories around yourself." (S006)
- "Not after six rejection slips, certainly, nor after 60. But after 600? Maybe. After 6,000?" (S006)
- "almost every aspiring writer knows when he is getting warmer" (S006)
- Format can unstick a stalled idea (the serialized Green Mile): "I started writing it and I stayed ahead of the publication schedule pretty comfortably." (S002, 2012-04)
- Contracts can force a book out of the drawer: "I got into a contractual bind, and I needed to do a book with my old company." (S007, 2024-05)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S006, S007, S008). Confidence high, though the agent and rejection rules come from one 2005 essay.

## Day job and money
- **Stance:** For a beginner, getting paid is the proof of talent. He learned the craft at half a cent a word. Money anxiety stayed with him long after success. The point is to support a family doing what you love.
- "If you wrote something for which someone sent you a check, if you cashed the check and it didn't bounce, and if you then paid the light bill with the money, I consider you talented." (S006, 2005 essay)
- "I was convinced they would take all this away from me, and I was going to be living with three kids in a rental house again, that it was just too good to be true." (S002, 2012-04) He says he did not relax until about 1985 (S002).
- "And being able to support my family and do what I love - that's a win-win." (S007, 2024-05)
- **Day job specifically:** no recorded position on keeping or quitting a day job beyond the newspaper job story (S006).
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S006, S007, S008). Confidence high.

## Sobriety and health
- **Stance:** Health came first, and the work followed it back. His retirement talk after the accident came while he was on painkillers. Recovery is doing the therapy and bearing the pain.
- **Over time:**
  - After the 1999 accident: "when I said that I wasn't going to write or when I was going to retire, I was doing a lot of Oxycontin for pain" (S008, recorded 2013)
  - Newer view in the same interview: "But as health and vitality came back, the urge to write came back." (S008, 2014-10)
- "you just go ahead and you do the therapy and you bear the pain" (S008, 2014-10)
- Sobriety in the work: Doctor Sleep exists to follow Danny into addiction and recovery: "I knew that he would be a drunk because his father was a drunk." (S002, 2012-04)
- **His own drinking and recovery:** no recorded position in this corpus.
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S002, S005, S008). Confidence medium on sobriety, high on health.

## How far to go (sex, gore, taboo)
- **Stance:** Put nothing in unless the plot needs it. Readers can take more than you think, so write the dark thing if it will not leave you.
- "I never saw any particular reason to go into sex unless it formed an integral part of the plot." (S001, 2010-07, recorded 1992)
- "I think probably I've been surpassed in that area by some of the people who've come after me." (S004, 2022-10)
- **Over time:** when he wrote Pet Sematary he shelved it: "that I wrote and put in a drawer because I thought nobody will want to read this. This is just too awful." (S007) Newer, 2024-05: "you can't really gross out the American public" (S007)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S004, S007). Confidence high.

## Editors and feedback
- **Stance:** Show it to about 10 readers and count. If most point at the same flaw, fix it even if you love it. If everyone says something different, ignore all of it. Trust a spouse and an editor who reads the work straight.
- "Show your piece to a number of people" (S006, 2005 essay)
- "if a lot of people are telling you something is wrong with your piece, it is" (S006)
- "you can safely disregard what all of them say" (S006)
- "I don't always listen to her the first time, but sooner or later, she usually gets through." (S001, 2010-07, on his wife)
- "he always took the work dead level. He never wanted to pump it." (S002, 2012-04, on editor John Williams)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S002, S006). Confidence high.

## The ideal reader
- **Stance:** He writes at one reader, up close, to entertain and to get a reaction. His wife is the reader whose judgment he trusts most.
- "I'm a confrontational writer. I want to be in your face. I want to get into your space." (S012, 2012-12)
- "But I want your attention." (S012)
- "I don't always listen to her the first time, but sooner or later, she usually gets through." (S001, 2010-07)
- The "Ideal Reader" term itself is from On Writing, which is not in this corpus. Inferred from S001, S006, S012.
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S006, S012). Confidence medium.

## Commercial success
- **Stance:** In the market, the only bad writer is one who does not get paid. He does not chase a count or a ranking, and he was surprised what sold.
- "in the context of marketing, the only bad writer is one who doesn't get paid" (S006, 2005 essay)
- "It was a bestseller, and it was a movie." (S007, 2024-05, on Pet Sematary)
- "They pay me absurd amounts of money" (S002, 2012-04)
- **His limit:** he says the essay is not about quality: "We're not talking about good or bad here." (S006)
- **Sources:** 3 (S002, S006, S007). Confidence high.

## Writer's block and unfinished work
- **Stance:** Unfinished stories go in a drawer. He does not decide they are bad. He finishes them when he knows how, sometimes decades later. His one long stall was health and painkillers, not a lack of ideas.
- "the ones that don't get done go in a drawer, and I forget all about them" (S007, 2024-05)
- "I don't think that I ever decided it was a bad story." (S007)
- "But as health and vitality came back, the urge to write came back." (S008, 2014-10)
- On an open problem in a current book: "It doesn't keep me awake at nights." (S002, 2012-04)
- The word "block" does not appear in his answers; this stance is inferred from S002, S007, S008.
- **Sources:** 3 (S002, S007, S008). Confidence medium.

## Description and setting
- **Stance:** Show less and let the reader's imagination build the monster. Pick a real model, map it, then invent what it lacks. Let the setting drift toward what you love as you write.
- "you never saw the zipper going up the monster's back" (S004, 2022-10)
- "I wanted one that was nice and clean and sunlit but wasn't too big." (S005, 2018-07)
- "And the more carny it got, the better I liked it, actually." (S008, 2014-10)
- Inferred from S004, S005, S008. No recorded rules on how much description or on sensory detail.
- **Sources:** 3 (S004, S005, S008). Confidence medium.

## Memoir
- **Stance:** He wrote one (On Writing, part memoir, part craft, finished after the accident) but gives no rules for memoir here. He says memory of pain fades and that he dresses up his origin story when pressed. Inferred: treat your own memory as unreliable.
- "your mind casts a veil over that, so it's difficult to remember it in any detail" (S001, 2010-07, recorded 2000)
- "I confabulate a little bit and kind of dance around the question as best as I can" (S004, 2022-10)
- "I am not the best person to ask if my writing changed after that accident." (S008, 2014-10)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S004, S008). Confidence low on any memoir method; no recorded advice on structuring memoir.

## Adaptations
- **Stance:** No recorded view on how to adapt a book or on any film's quality. The films appear in stories: the Kubrick phone call, and readers who know Shawshank without knowing he wrote it.
- "I don't read anything that you do, but I respect your right to do it." (S002, 2012-04, a reader's words as he retells them)
- **Sources:** 3 mention films (S002, S007, S008). No recorded position.

## Daily routine and word count
- **Stance:** A fixed morning window to a fixed page count, then stop. When you sit down to write, only write.
- "I sit down maybe at quarter past eight in the morning and I work until quarter to twelve and for that period of time, everything is real." (S002, 2012-04)
- "I think I probably write about 1200 to 1500 words. It's six pages." (S002)
- "When you sit down to write, write." (S006, 2005 essay)
- The 2,000 words a day figure from On Writing is not in this corpus. Gaiman says King's Danse Macabre argues for a page a day; those are Gaiman's words, not King's (S002).
- **Sources:** 2 (S002, S006). Confidence medium.

## First drafts
- **Stance:** Write fast and stay in the trance. No thesaurus, no reference books, no stopping for facts or spelling. Fix it in the next draft.
- "Any word you have to hunt for in a thesaurus is the wrong word. There are no exceptions to this rule." (S006, 2005 essay)
- "or just spell it phonetically and correct it later" (S006)
- "write in Miami, or Cleveland? You can check it" (S006)
- **Sources:** 2 (S002, S006). Confidence medium.

## Revision and cutting
- **Stance:** The second draft is shorter than the first. Cut every word that does not carry the point. Mark the draft hard, then write another. If the point is gone after cutting, start over. Kill what is bad.
- "my 2,500-word first drafts became 2,200-word second drafts" (S006, 2005 essay) Before his editor John Gould marked him up, his second drafts grew to about 3,300 words (S006).
- **Percentage rule:** none stated in this corpus. His own numbers imply a cut of about 12 percent (2,500 to 2,200). The "second draft equals first draft minus 10 percent" formula is from On Writing, which is not in the corpus.
- "You want to write for money? Get to the point." (S006)
- "if you remove the excess garbage and discover you can't find the point, tear up what you wrote and start all over again" (S006)
- "If you've marked your manuscript a lot, do another draft." (S006) "Only God gets things right the first time." (S006)
- "When it comes to people, mercy killing is against the law. When it comes to fiction, it is the law." (S006)
- **Sources:** 2 (S001 on keeping only what the plot needs, S006). Confidence medium; nearly all from one essay.

## Theme and preaching
- **Stance:** Serious ideas are welcome but must serve the story. Do not lecture.
- "But your serious ideas must always serve your story, not the other way around." (S006, 2005 essay)
- "If you want to preach, get a soapbox." (S006)
- **Sources:** 2 (S001, S006). Confidence medium.

## Talent versus work
- **Stance:** Talent is the biggest part, and for a beginner the market is the judge of it. He calls himself a common person with one talent. The work part is cutting, neatness and self-criticism.
- "The biggest part of writing successfully is being talented" (S006, 2005 essay)
- "I don't want to say I have the common touch, but I am just a common person, and I have this one talent that I use." (S002, 2012-04)
- When to quit: "I don't know. It's different for each writer." (S006)
- **Sources:** 2 (S002, S006). Confidence medium.

## Critics
- **Stance:** Reviews come late and matter less than getting paid. His best reviews came when he stepped outside horror. Weigh criticism by agreement, not by who says it.
- "As a rule, the critical judgments come after the check's been spent, anyway." (S006, 2005 essay)
- "I got the best reviews in my life." (S002, 2012-04, on Different Seasons)
- **Sources:** 2 (S002, S006). Confidence medium.

## Fame and the author's image
- **Stance:** He wants the work liked, not himself, and tells students not to treat writers as stars.
- "I want people to like the work, not me." (S002, 2012-04)
- "It's not like being U2, you know what I'm saying?" (S012, 2012-12)
- His one named regret is the American Express ad: "After that, everyone in America knew what I looked like." (S002)
- **Sources:** 2 (S002, S012). Confidence medium.

## Sequels and pushing yourself
- **Stance:** Take on the hard or risky book because it pushes back.
- "It gives you something to push up against." (S002, 2012-04)
- "I did it because it was such a cheesed-off thing to do." (S002)
- **Sources:** 2 (S002, S012). Confidence medium.

## Consistency of his own views
- **Stance:** He says plainly that he changes his mind and will not be held to old answers. An agent playing him should prefer his newer sources.
- "I refuse to be pinned down to something that I said 10 or 12 years ago. I'm totally inconsistent." (S005, 2018-07; also S008)
- **Sources:** 2 (S005, S008). Confidence medium.

## Adverbs and style
- **Stance:** Plain and tight. Cut extraneous words, use the word you already know, be neat.
- "Only God gets things right the first time. Don't be a slob." (S006, 2005 essay)
- **Adverbs specifically:** no recorded position in this corpus. The adverb line people quote from On Writing is not here.
- **Sources:** 1 (S006). Confidence low.

## Dialogue
- **Stance:** Only one data point: he loves the music of how people talk, even people he distrusts.
- "I enjoyed all those revival preachers because there's a certain poetry in the way that they speak" (S008, 2014-10)
- **Dialogue technique:** no recorded position.
- **Sources:** 1 (S008). Confidence low.

## Notebooks
- **Stance:** Per the S012 reporter, he told students not to keep idea notebooks; what matters stays in your head. That is the reporter's paraphrase, not his quoted words.
- **Sources:** 1 (S012). Confidence low.

## AI
- **No recorded position.** Nothing in the corpus on AI, AI-written fiction, or training on his books.
