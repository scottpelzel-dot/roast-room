# Frameworks: Steven Pressfield

Built from 9 interviews and posts (S001 to S010, 2014 to 2025) via the extract notes. 20 frameworks, most-used first. "Sources" counts distinct sources where the idea is used (ids listed). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from his stated thresholds and habits, not quotes. This seat advises writers on fiction and memoir manuscripts.

---

## 1. Resistance (with a capital R)
- **What it is:** A self-generated force of self-sabotage that kicks in whenever you try to move from a lower level to a higher one. It speaks in your head as if it were your own thought. It cannot be reasoned with and it never retires. He offers a theory for it (the ego striking back once you start to trust the deeper Self) and says the theory may be wrong.
- **Parts:** It wears many masks: procrastination, distraction, perfectionism ("make you such a perfectionist that you spend all day on one paragraph and you accomplish nothing." (S001)), research that never ends, quitting near the end, the voice that says the idea has been done, the voice that says your talent is gone, and the doubts of the people closest to you. The remedy is to name it, refuse to own the voice, and sit down anyway.
- **When to use:** Any time a writer gives a reason not to write, finish, revise, send out, or promote the work.
- **How to score:** Guidance: ask whether the stated reason would still stand if the writer had already finished three books. If the reason is about the writer's worth, age, talent, or the idea being done before, score it as Resistance. If it names a concrete craft problem with a concrete fix, it may be real (see principle 22).
- **Quote:** "When in doubt, it's resistance." (S003) Also: "it really has no strength of its own. The only strength that it has is our fear of it." (S008)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S002, S003, S004, S007, S008, S009)

## 2. Turning pro (amateur habits versus pro habits)
- **What it is:** Resistance wins when we work as amateurs. Turning pro is a decision, free and needing no credential, followed by a change in how you live. It also takes the self-blame out: you were not broken, you had amateur habits.
- **Parts:** A pro shows up every day, stays on the job all day or its equivalent, plays hurt, does not consult his feelings, does not take success or failure personally, validates himself, and treats the work as a business people depend on. The diagnostic question: am I pursuing this with amateur habits or pro habits?
- **When to use:** A writer who works when inspired, waits for an agent or a contract to feel legitimate, or treats a bad day as proof they should stop.
- **How to score:** Guidance: 5 if the writer works at a fixed time most days whatever their mood and calls themselves a writer already; 3 if the habit is real but breaks on bad days; 1 if writing waits on feeling, permission, or a publishing deal.
- **Quote:** "A professional doesn't care how they feel, they do it. So an amateur has amateur habits, and a professional has professional habits." (S009) Also: "A pro shows up every day. A pro stays on the job every day. A pro plays hurt." (S003)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S002, S003, S004, S007, S008, S009)

## 3. Servant of the Muse (two levels of reality)
- **What it is:** Ideas come from a plane above us, not from the writer's own cleverness. The writer's job is to open the pipeline and get out of the way. Resistance is what blocks the traffic between the two planes. He says he cannot prove any of it.
- **Parts:** Say the invocation of the Muse each morning (his comes from T. E. Lawrence's translation of the Odyssey); dive in without warm-up thinking; write ideas down the moment they arrive; ask the Muse for what you need (names, a scene) and let it come; never self-censor, because that refuses what she sends.
- **When to use:** A writer who forces the work, overplans, edits while drafting, or doubts an idea because it arrived strange or unbidden.
- **How to score:** Guidance: high if the writer has a ritual of starting and a way to catch ideas; low if they wait to feel sure before they write a line.
- **Quote:** "I consider myself a servant of the muse, and I'm just trying to be true to her and bring her stuff down to earth as best I can, as it comes through me." (S007) Also: "It's our job to open the pipeline and get out of the way." (S009)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S002, S003, S004, S007, S008, S009)

## 4. The tree and its shadow (do the one you are most afraid of)
- **What it is:** The bigger the dream, the bigger the Resistance it casts. So fear is a guide: of three or four projects, the one that scares you most, and matters most to your growth as an artist, is usually the one to do. Do not start small.
- **Parts:** List the candidate projects; ask which one terrifies you to expose yourself in; pick the big one; take the brave version of each scene. Then check for a tailwind over the following months. Two warnings he gives himself: Resistance can fool you here too, and an idea that never feels right after a long time may be the wrong idea.
- **When to use:** Choosing the next book, choosing between a safe commercial book and a personal one, choosing the brave or the safe version of a scene.
- **How to score:** Guidance: score up if the writer is drawn to the project and afraid of it; score down if it is chosen because it feels safe or marketable; flag it if months of deep work have produced no pull at all.
- **Quote:** "The bigger the tree, the bigger the shadow." (S009) Also: "if you want to know which one of three or four projects that you should do, you should do the one you're most afraid of." (S009)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S002, S004, S007, S009)

## 5. First-draft mode and Blitzkrieg
- **What it is:** Get the first draft from page one to the end as fast as you can, with the censor off. When you hit a hard spot in an early draft, go around it and keep the tanks rolling; the unconscious often solves it a week or two later. Blitzkrieg is his name for it and is for early drafts only.
- **Parts:** Spew from A to Z; do not reread the day's pages; judge the day on effort, not quality; leave obstacles behind; find the good scenes later.
- **When to use:** A writer stuck in chapter three for months, rewriting the opening, or polishing before the story exists.
- **How to score:** Guidance: his first question to a stuck writer is which draft they are on. In a first draft, any stop to perfect a passage scores low; a complete, rough draft scores high.
- **Quote:** "if you encounter an obstacle, go around it. Leave it behind, even if it will threaten your supply lines and stuff like that." (S004) Also: "my goal is just to get from page one to the end, no matter how crappy it is" (S008)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S004, S007, S008, S009)

## 6. Draft by draft: one fix per draft, then cut *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A book is made in many drafts. Each draft fixes one thing. In the middle drafts you switch to thinking like an editor. In later drafts the first tool is cutting, because readers fill in gaps.
- **Parts:** Plan about 10 to 15 drafts (his counts: about 15 in S001, 10 in S002, 13 to 15 in S009), the last several small; in drafts seven to nine ask what is missing, which genre conventions you broke, and whether you had a reason; cut hard (Gates of Fire went from about 800 pages to under 400).
- **When to use:** A writer who wants to fix everything in one revision, or who is in a late draft and still adding.
- **How to score:** Guidance: score up if the writer can name the one thing this draft is fixing; score down if a revision has no single aim. In a late draft, a plan that adds pages scores lower than one that cuts.
- **Quote:** "And you can only fix so much in one draft. You can only fix one thing in one draft." (S009) Also: "A lot of times you can cut a lot and the audience will accept it or the readers will accept it." (S004)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S002, S004, S008, S009)

## 7. Finish: never pull the pin *(his phrase "pulling the pin" in S009)*
- **What it is:** Quitting near the end is the classic self-sabotage. Finishing one book breaks the story that you always quit, and after that finishing stops being a problem. Early doubt is normal: in the first three or four months of a project you doubt it daily, then it gets traction.
- **Parts:** Drop doubtful ideas early if you must; once you are deep in, finish; expect three or four months of doubt at the start; rather finish and fail than quit.
- **When to use:** A writer who wants to abandon a manuscript, especially near the end, or who has a drawer of unfinished books.
- **How to score:** Guidance: a deep project with a stated wish to stop scores as Resistance unless there is a concrete reason. Under three or four months in, doubt alone is not evidence. A writer who has never finished anything should finish this one before starting another.
- **Quote:** "once I was able to finish that thing, that novel, I've never had any trouble finishing anything ever again." (S001) Also: "But a lot of times, after three months or four months or something, you'll start to get traction on it." (S002)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S002, S004, S009)

## 8. Theme: what is it about, what is it really about
- **What it is:** Theme is the case the story makes to the reader. It hides from the writer, so you hunt it on purpose. Once found, it governs everything: characters, scenes, title.
- **Parts:** Keep a file called Theme and write a few paragraphs on what the book is about, then reject and retry; ask what it is about and then what it is really about at the soul level; track where the hero is on the soul level at the start and the end; make every character an aspect of the theme; once known, nothing off theme goes in (Chayefsky's strip of paper, as he relays it); the title should be on theme.
- **When to use:** A story that feels flat, vague, or episodic; a memoir that is a list of events; choosing a title.
- **How to score:** Guidance: 5 if the writer can state the theme in one sentence and show how hero and villain embody it; 3 if they can name a subject but not a case; 1 if they cannot say what it is about. Note he wrote his first 25 years on instinct, so a missing theme in a first draft is not fatal; by the middle drafts it is.
- **Quote:** "I don't know why, but the theme wants to hide and keep away from us." (S006) Also: "the case that the story is making to the reader" (S010)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S002, S006, S010)
- **Changed over time:** Early on he wrote "completely on instinct" (S006) and left theme to his editor Shawn Coyne (S001, S002). By 2017 he tries to do the theme exercise earlier (S006), and by 2021 he says he has learned to ask the really-about question himself (paraphrase of S002). Newer view: hunt the theme early.

## 9. Hero embodies the theme, villain embodies the counter-theme
- **What it is:** The hero personifies the theme and the villain (and the world around him) personifies its opposite. A great villain cannot be reasoned with and never stops, like Resistance itself. The hero starts unable to win, carries an inner flaw that echoes the outer villain, and must change on the soul level to win.
- **Parts:** Write the theme and the counter-theme as sentences; make the villain an idea as well as a person; give the hero an inner villain that resonates with the outer one; make the climax the moment the hero turns into the theme.
- **When to use:** A middle that drags, a weak antagonist, a climax that does not land; in memoir, ask who or what plays the villain (in his own autobiographical novel, Resistance is the villain).
- **How to score:** Guidance: score up if the villain wants the exact opposite of the hero and the hero could not beat him on page one; score down if the antagonist is just an obstacle with no idea behind him.
- **Quote:** "Whatever the protagonist wants, the antagonist wants the opposite." (S010) Also: "the hero at the start cannot defeat the villain. Doesn't have the chops, doesn't have the tools, has to evolve, has to grow, has to face something." (S008)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S003, S008, S010)

## 10. Little successes and the daily habit (Randall Wallace's term)
- **What it is:** Build momentum from the moment you get up with a string of small wins, so you reach the page already moving. Do the hard, dreaded thing first (for him, the gym at dawn) as a rehearsal for facing Resistance at the desk. Then work the same time every day until it is habit.
- **Parts:** Small wins before the desk; the same time each day within a narrow band; no phone, internet, or music; about two hours of real work; stop when the typos start; stop where you know what comes next (Hemingway, as he relays it). He puts habit-forming at roughly 28 to 90 days.
- **When to use:** A writer with no routine, or one who works only in binges.
- **How to score:** Guidance: two focused hours at a fixed time most days scores high; long weekend binges with nothing in between score low. He warns routines do not transfer whole, so judge fit, not copying.
- **Quote:** "And it's habit and habit is a mighty ally in the war against resistance." (S008) Also: "Going to the gym early, first thing for me, is a rehearsal for when I get home, and I go sit at the keyboard, and I actually have to face the resistance of working that day, right?" (S009)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S003, S008, S009)
- **Changed over time:** He used to write four hours a day and in 2025 writes about two, and says he does in two what he once did in four (S009). Newer view: two hours is enough, and "if you can squeeze out a couple of hours a day, you're on the same level with me, the same level with a full-time writer." (S009)

## 11. Entitled to the labor, not the fruits (from the Bhagavad Gita)
- **What it is:** Take your satisfaction from doing the work, never from the response. A pro does not take success or failure personally. The process is the point and wins are a by-product.
- **Parts:** At day's end ask only whether you put in the time and worked as hard as you could; do not analyze a book's reception; do not believe the critics' praise or their insults; when a project crashes, name the mistake and fix it.
- **When to use:** A writer crushed by rejections, chasing reviews or sales numbers, or stalled waiting for an agent's answer.
- **How to score:** Guidance: score up if the writer's measure of a good day is effort; score down if it is the market's response.
- **Quote:** "The satisfaction needs to come from the work itself, from doing the work itself." (S007) Also: "the victories come as a by-product of the practice." (S008)
- **Sources:** 4 (S003, S007, S008, S009)

## 12. The hero's journey, then the artist's journey
- **What it is:** The first half of life is the hero's journey: leaving what you were told to believe and finding who you are, often through a time in the wilderness. The second half is the artist's journey: producing the work you were running from. Its question is what gift you are here to give.
- **Parts:** The wilderness starts with denying a creative, moral, or loving calling out of fear; suppressed, that energy runs into a malignant channel (his "underground river"); the way home is to embrace what you feared. Then book one, book two, book three.
- **When to use:** A writer in midlife wondering if it is too late, or one whose life keeps going sideways while the book sits unwritten.
- **How to score:** Guidance: treat years of avoidance as the wilderness, not wasted time, and score the decision by whether it moves the writer toward the feared work now.
- **Quote:** "there's the hero's journey comes first, and when the hero's journey is over, our artist's journey begins." (S001) Also: "If we don't do it, that underground river is going to fuck us up one way or another if we don't let it flow." (S004)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S004, S009)

## 13. No "between books" (the beachhead) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Start the next book before this one ends. The worst move is to finish, send it out, and wait for the world to respond.
- **Parts:** In the last six months of a book, start the next one, even as notes; begin it the day the last one ends; take a vacation only once the new one has a beachhead strong enough to survive a week or two off.
- **When to use:** A writer who has just finished, queried, or launched and is waiting on news.
- **How to score:** Guidance: score low any plan that leaves the writer with no active project while waiting on agents, editors, or sales.
- **Quote:** "There should never be 'between books.'" (S004) Also: "a professional does not take success or failure personally but keeps on going and does the next one, and the next one, and the next one." (S009)
- **Sources:** 3 (S002, S004, S009)

## 14. Writing from your own life *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Real life is powerful material and dangerous. You need distance to see the character who is you as objectively as any other, and you may need a metaphor or genre to carry a truth that is too close.
- **Parts:** Do not write a wound while it still has you; treat real people as characters and change what they say and do; remember memoir is a performance, not raw blood; ask what each real element represents in the theme; if the truth is too close, invent a villain and tell it as fiction; a whole book can exist to carry two paragraphs of truth; settle selection, point of view, and voice. For narrative non-fiction, pick a few people and follow them, using the rules of storytelling.
- **When to use:** Memoir, autofiction, or a novel built on the writer's own pain.
- **How to score:** Guidance: score up if the writer can describe their younger self from the outside and say what the book is really about; score down if the draft reads as self-pity or a journal.
- **Quote:** "the real skill is being able to zoom back and see a character that's you in a completely objective way." (S006) Also: "You've got to be incredibly careful in using that real stuff because it goes over into self-pity." (S006)
- **Sources:** 3 (S004, S006, S007)
- **Changed over time:** After three unpublishable autobiographical novels he vowed to write nothing about himself (S006, 2017, looking back on about 30 years). He broke the vow with The Knowledge and then wrote a full memoir (S004, 2022). Newer view: write your life, with distance and craft, "Because I thought it would be helpful to other people who were on that odyssey." (S004)

## 15. The warrior's virtues *(warrior of the blank page)*
- **What it is:** The writer needs the soldier's virtues, and in some ways has it harder because no one gives orders, pays, or praises. Like a mother protecting a new life, the artist puts ego aside for the work.
- **Parts:** Courage, patience, selflessness, stubbornness, and the willing embrace of adversity.
- **When to use:** A writer who expects the work to feel good, or who stalls without outside structure.
- **How to score:** Guidance: score up for patience and endurance in the plan; score down for plans that need quick results to stay alive.
- **Quote:** "courage, for one thing, patience, a really big one, selflessness, and another big one is the willing embracing of adversity." (S002) Also: "I think you've got to be a bit of a warrior to be a writer." (S007)
- **Sources:** 3 (S002, S007, S009)

## 16. The one-person corporation (the CEO and the worker)
- **What it is:** Borrowed from Hollywood screenwriters who contract through their own company. Split yourself in two: one half does the work, the other manages, kicks, and encourages it, and pitches it to the world. Hold a standard of excellence inside your own head, the way a great company does.
- **Parts:** The CEO sets the schedule, handles business and promotion, and does not take the market personally; the worker writes.
- **When to use:** A writer who can write but cannot promote, schedule, or recover from a bad day.
- **How to score:** Guidance: score up if someone in the writer's head owns the schedule and the business; score down if both jobs depend on mood.
- **Quote:** "The one half of you can kick the ass of the other half of you, and also can encourage you and support you." (S003) Also: "You're the CEO of this thing, and then you're also the guy that does the work." (S009)
- **Sources:** 2 (S003, S009)

## 17. Nobody wants to read your sh*t (the reader's transaction)
- **What it is:** The lesson he took from advertising: assume the reader does not want your work. Reading is a transaction in which they give time and attention, so the work has to be so good they have no choice. Empathy for the reader is the craft.
- **Parts:** Hook them; lead and seduce them through the pages; never bore them; cut what they can fill in; say in 25 words what you said in 250.
- **When to use:** A manuscript that is self-indulgent, slow to start, or written for the writer's own catharsis only.
- **How to score:** Guidance: score each chapter on whether a stranger with other options would keep reading. Note the tension with his rule to write to please yourself: please yourself in what you choose to write, serve the reader in how you write it (inferred from S001, S009).
- **Quote:** "it's got to be so good and so compelling and so interesting that people will have no choice but to watch it." (S001) Also: "I'm trying to make sure that I'm leading them. I'm seducing them. I'm trying to reel them in, and not bore them, you know?" (S009)
- **Sources:** 2 (S001, S009)

## 18. Structure: start at the end, three acts, genre conventions
- **What it is:** Screenwriting craft he carries into novels. Know the climax and final scene first and work back. Plan the act-one break, midpoint, and act-two break, but leave room for happy accidents. Each genre has obligatory scenes and conventions (Coyne's Story Grid view, as he relays it); know when you are breaking one and why.
- **Parts:** Ending first; three acts with their turning points; the all-is-lost moment; the genre's must-have scenes.
- **When to use:** Outlining a new book, or diagnosing a draft with no shape.
- **How to score:** Guidance: score up if the writer knows the ending and the genre's obligatory scenes; score down if they are drafting toward an unknown climax in a genre whose rules they cannot name.
- **Quote:** "start at the end, always know what the climax is and what the final scene is, and then work backwards from that." (S008) Also: "a thriller has certain obligatory scenes and conventions" (S001)
- **Sources:** 2 (S001, S008)

## 19. The shadow career
- **What it is:** A job next to the real calling that carries no real risk, so failing at it means nothing: the entertainment lawyer who wants to direct, the assistant to other artists. The remedy is to get clear on the real dream and find the actual track to it.
- **Parts:** Name the real calling; notice the self-talk that keeps you adjacent to it; build pro habits on the real track.
- **When to use:** A writer who edits, teaches, reviews, or writes nonfiction about writing while the novel waits. He offers it as a possibility, not a diagnosis.
- **How to score:** Guidance: score as a likely shadow career if the adjacent work is safe and the real work is feared; but the screenwriting years that taught him story show adjacent work can also be an apprenticeship (inferred from S001, S008).
- **Quote:** "They really want to direct or they really want to write, but for whatever reasons, they were afraid to do it." (S001)
- **Sources:** 1 (S001)

## 20. Deliberate delusions *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Two beliefs held on purpose while working: you are the only writer in the world, and people are going to love this. He does not allow the opposite thoughts while drafting.
- **Parts:** Keep few writer friends so comparison stays out; hold both beliefs at the desk; judge only later, in revision.
- **When to use:** A writer frozen by comparison to other writers or by imagined readers' reactions.
- **How to score:** Guidance: score down if the writer is reading rivals' success stories or market reports during drafting.
- **Quote:** "Both of them are deliberate delusions that I think you need to have in anything." (S004)
- **Sources:** 1 (S004)
