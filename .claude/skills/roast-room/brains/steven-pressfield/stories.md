# Stories: Steven Pressfield

Built from the extract notes on 9 sources (S001 to S010). 16 stories and cases he reuses, with the lesson he draws. Most-used first. Source ids in parentheses.

1. **The first novel he quit at the finish line.** He abandoned his first novel a hundred feet from the end and wrecked his marriage instead. He finished the next one by will and shame: "I'm going to finish this son of a bitch or I'm going to kill myself." (S001) Typing "The end" felt like a change in his DNA. Lesson: finish once and the story that you always quit is broken for good (S001, S004, S009).
2. **Hugh Reeves and the dropped trailer.** As a young trucker he dropped a $300,000 trailer through self-sabotage. His boss took him for a hot dog and told him he was a professional driver and to deliver the load. Lesson: the job is not a stage for your inner drama; "You're a professional person. Do it. Whatever it takes. Do it." (S004) (S001, S004, S009).
3. **Three failed novels, then Hollywood.** About 13 years of novels that failed, supported by odd jobs, then Los Angeles, where it took six or seven more years to earn a penny. He calls the movies a PhD in story. Lesson: failure in one form is the apprenticeship for the next, and turning pro does not bring fast money (S001, S003, S006, S008).
4. **The War of Art: the slow burn.** Friends kept saying they had a book in them and never wrote it; what he told them became The War of Art. He thought only writers would care. It "pooped around for like about 10 years" (S008) before it found readers, and Gates of Fire took about ten more. Lesson: a book can find its audience a decade late (S002, S007, S008, S009).
5. **Paul Rink, the mentor in the camper.** An older writer had him to coffee every morning, told him fiction is a full-time war, told him to finish the book and start the next one tomorrow, and typed out the invocation of the Muse he has said every morning for about 50 years. Lesson: a mentor's standard and a daily ritual carry a writer (S004, S009).
6. **Bagger Vance: the book his agent told him not to write.** His Hollywood agent dropped him when he chose a golf novel over screenplays. The book came fast, sold fast, and became a movie fast; later the producer fired him from the film. Lesson: follow the idea that seizes you, even against expert advice. He puts his age then at 53 or 54 (S008); Huberman's intro says 52 (S009) (S001, S003, S008).
7. **King Kong Lives.** His screenplay flopped and a review mocked the writers' names. Friends sat in silence at the screening, and one told him he was in the arena. Lesson: a public failure keeps you humble; do not study it, move on (S008, S009).
8. **Thirty-six Righteous Men vanished.** Three years of work, no promotion, and the book disappeared. He thought his career might be over, then learned marketing from Ryan Holiday and Jack Carr for A Man at Arms. Lesson: the book was not the problem, awareness was; and Resistance hits marketing too (S002, S008).
9. **Gates of Fire cut in half.** The first draft ran 802 pages and the book 384 (S001), or 800 and 375 (S008). The book came easily; the cutting was the technical work. Lesson: write it all, then cut hard; readers fill gaps (S001, S008).
10. **Telamon, the character who arrived whole.** A minor character appeared unplanned, fully formed, with a dark philosophy, in two books a century apart. Years of outlines for his own book never caught fire until he added a young, vulnerable girl to his world. Lesson: let characters tell you what they think, and keep outlining until one catches (S001, S002, S008).
11. **The Knowledge and Teaspoon the cat.** His "too close to true" novel of 1970s New York exists to carry two true paragraphs about a crime he could not write for 35 years. A real cat by his typewriter became Teaspoon, who stands for the Muse; Coyne said the cat had to be kidnapped. Lesson: ask what each real element represents, and build a story around the true core (S003, S006).
12. **Ed Hannibal quits advertising.** His ad-agency boss quit to write a novel that became a hit. Pressfield thought he could do the same and quit, then went about 30 years before a novel was published. Lesson: a role model shows you the door, but not how long the hall is (S008, S009).
13. **The Lion's Gate, the book he avoided.** After many books on Greeks and others, he realized he had never written about his own people. He dreaded the Six-Day War book because it would change him, and did it for that reason. He interviewed 67 or so veterans and let one recurring family tragedy become a main thread. Lesson: the project you avoid may be the one to do, and nonfiction obeys story rules (S007).
14. **Delivering government cheese.** He hauled surplus government cheese to churches on the North Carolina coast for $15 a run. "I didn't grow the cheese. All I did was deliver it. When I'm done, the trailer is empty." (S004) Lesson: the writer delivers what the Muse provides, then leaves (S004).
15. **The manuscript that never left the box.** A friend wrote a deeply personal novel and could not mail it; he died with it unsent. "I don't know whether that was perfectionism or just fear of being judged in the real world." (S009) Lesson: unshipped work is the costliest failure (S009).
16. **Rick in Casablanca.** Rick starts out sticking his neck out for no one and ends by putting Ilsa on the plane for the cause. The villain is the idea of safe neutrality. Lesson: the hero's arc is turning into the theme, and the villain embodies the counter-theme (S006, S010).
